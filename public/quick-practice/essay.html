i Orientation

How to use this guide

The descriptive paper is not a test of how much you know. It is a test of whether you can organise what you know, in a fixed form, under time pressure. Every mark in précis, letters and essays goes to structure, brevity and control before it goes to content.

So this guide is built the same way each time: first the method (the rules and the moves that earn marks), then fully worked answers you can read like worked sums. Read a method once, then study the model answer beside it and notice why each choice was made — the subject line, the topic sentence, the word count. Then close the guide and reproduce it in your own hand. Imitation first, originality later.

The single habit that lifts scores

Before you write a single word of any answer, spend 60–90 seconds making a skeleton in the margin — the title/subject, and 3–5 points in order. Most lost marks in this paper come not from weak knowledge but from answers that wander, repeat, or run out of time. A 90-second plan prevents all three.

1 Module One

Précis writing

What a précis is — and the rules that score

A précis is a compression of a passage to about one-third of its length, in your own words, keeping every essential idea and dropping everything else. It is the discipline of saying the same thing in fewer, better-chosen words. The examiner is checking three things: did you grasp the core, did you cut ruthlessly, and is it still readable English?

The non-negotiable rules

  1. Length ≈ one-third. If the passage is ~210 words, aim for ~70. Count your words and write the count at the end (e.g. "Words: 68"). Staying within ±10% of one-third is itself worth marks.
  2. One continuous paragraph. No headings, no bullet points, no sub-divisions — however long the original.
  3. Your own words. Do not lift whole sentences. Re-phrase. Replace "the discharge of chemicals, sewage and industrial waste" with "untreated waste". Lifting is penalised even when accurate.
  4. Third person, reported tone. Drop direct address, rhetorical questions and the author's "we/you". Keep it neutral and objective.
  5. No new matter, no opinion, no examples of your own. You report only what the passage says — but you may merge several examples into one summary word (e.g. "traffic, machinery and loud activity" → "noise sources").
  6. A suitable title. Short, relevant, capturing the theme — placed at the top.

The method — four passes

  1. Read twice — once for the gist, once with a pencil underlining the controlling idea of each paragraph.
  2. List the skeleton in the margin: theme + main points, ignoring repetition, illustrations and decorative phrases.
  3. Draft from the skeleton, not from the passage — this stops you copying. Link the points into flowing sentences.
  4. Trim and count. Cut adjectives, "very/really", and redundant pairs ("each and every"). Confirm the count and add the title.
High-yield trimming moves
  • Turn lists into a category: "air, water, soil, noise and light pollution" can stay as the list (it is the structure) but their long effects collapse into "harms health, ecosystems and resources".
  • Cut every phrase that only repeats or emphasises ("It is quite challenging… however, it is not unmanageable" → "difficult but possible").
  • Prefer one strong verb over a verb-plus-noun ("makes a contribution to" → "contributes").

Solved précis 1 — the Pollution passage

Question · reduce to one-third and give a suitable title (10)

Pollution is a global challenge that poses a threat to the health of our planet and its inhabitants. It manifests in various forms, including air, water, soil, noise and light pollution. The consequences of pollution are far-reaching, affecting ecosystems, biodiversity and human well-being. Industrial activities, urbanization and improper waste management contribute significantly to pollution.

Air pollution, caused by the release of pollutants into the atmosphere, leads to respiratory diseases, climate change and environmental degradation. Water pollution results from the discharge of chemicals, sewage and industrial waste into rivers, lakes and oceans, harming aquatic life and contaminating drinking water sources. Soil pollution occurs when pollutants like pesticides and heavy metals degrade the quality of soil, affecting plant growth and food safety.

Noise pollution, generated by traffic, industrial machinery and other human activities, can have adverse effects on human health, causing stress, sleep disturbances and hearing loss. Light pollution, caused by excessive artificial light in urban areas, disrupts natural ecosystems and interferes with the behaviour of nocturnal animals.

Addressing pollution requires collective efforts at individual, community and governmental levels. Individuals can contribute by adopting eco-friendly practices, reducing waste and using sustainable modes of transportation. Industries must invest in cleaner technologies and adhere to strict environmental standards.

Model Answer

Title: Pollution and Its Control

Pollution — air, water, soil, noise and light — is a worldwide problem harming ecosystems, biodiversity and human health, driven mainly by industry, urbanisation and poor waste disposal. Its various forms cause disease, environmental damage, contaminated water, degraded soil, and disturbances to people and wildlife. Tackling it demands joint action: individuals must adopt greener habits and reduce waste, while industries must use cleaner technology and obey strict environmental rules.

Words: 67 (original ≈ 205 · one-third ≈ 68)

Why it scores: it keeps the five forms (the passage's spine) but compresses three paragraphs of effects into one clause; it merges the solution paragraph into a single "individuals… industries…" contrast; nothing is lifted verbatim; and it lands almost exactly on one-third with the count shown.

Solved précis 2 — the Global Warming passage

Question · reduce to one-third and give a suitable title (10)

Global warming is a term almost everyone is familiar with. But its meaning is still not clear to most of us. So, global warming refers to the gradual rise in the overall temperature of the atmosphere of the Earth. There are various activities taking place which have been increasing the temperature gradually. Global warming is melting our ice glaciers rapidly. This is extremely harmful to the earth as well as its inhabitants. It is quite challenging to control global warming; however, it is not unmanageable. The first step in solving any problem is identifying the cause of the problem. Therefore, we need to first understand the causes of global warming that will help us proceed further in solving it.

These causes are both natural as well as manmade. The natural causes include the release of greenhouse gases which are not able to escape from earth, causing the temperature to increase. After that, the excessive use of automobiles and fossil fuels results in increased levels of carbon dioxide. In addition, activities like mining and cattle rearing are very harmful to the environment.

Global warming can be stopped when combined efforts are put in. For that, individuals and governments both have to take steps towards achieving it. We must begin with the reduction of greenhouse gas. An important step is to monitor the consumption of gasoline. Switch to a hybrid car and reduce the release of carbon dioxide. Moreover, citizens can choose public transport or carpool together. Subsequently, recycling must also be encouraged. On the government's part, they must regulate industrial waste and ban them from emitting harmful gases in the air. Deforestation must be stopped immediately and planting of trees must be encouraged. Global warming is a global problem, and therefore its solution also needs a global effort.

Model Answer

Title: Global Warming — Causes and Solutions

Global warming, though commonly mentioned, is poorly understood. It is the gradual rise in the Earth's atmospheric temperature caused by various activities, and it is rapidly melting glaciers, endangering the planet and its life. Controlling it is difficult but possible, and begins with identifying its natural and manmade causes — trapped greenhouse gases, heavy use of automobiles and fossil fuels, and mining and cattle rearing. Combined effort can curb it: individuals should cut greenhouse gases, limit fuel use, adopt hybrid cars, share transport and recycle, while governments must regulate industrial waste, ban harmful emissions, halt deforestation and promote tree planting. Being global, it needs a global response.

Words: 104 (original ≈ 300 · one-third ≈ 100)

Why it scores: the wandering opening ("a term almost everyone is familiar with… still not clear…") is reduced to four words; the two cause-types and the two actor-types (individual/government) are preserved as the backbone; and the closing line is kept because it is the passage's thesis, not decoration.

Solved précis 3 — "The Value of Time"

This is a self-made practice passage in the typical "reflective essay" style MPSC favours — drill it the same way as the two above.

Question · reduce to one-third and give a suitable title (10)

Time is the most valuable thing a person possesses, and yet it is the thing most carelessly wasted. Unlike money or property, time once lost can never be recovered. A wealthy man may regain his fortune and a sick man may recover his health, but no power on earth can bring back a single moment that has passed. This is what makes time so precious, and its waste so foolish.

Every great achievement in human history has been the fruit of time used wisely. The scholar who masters a subject, the athlete who wins a medal, the nation that rises to greatness — all have succeeded by valuing each hour and refusing to squander it. Successful people are not those who have more time than others, for everyone is given the same twenty-four hours in a day; they are those who use their time with purpose and discipline.

Wasting time, on the other hand, is a habit that ruins both individuals and societies. The person who idles away the present in laziness and delay finds, too late, that opportunities have slipped past and cannot be recalled. Procrastination, the thief of time, robs us of our dreams.

To value time is therefore to value life itself, for our life is nothing but the sum of the hours we are given. The wise make every moment count.

Model Answer

Title: The Value of Time

Time is a person's most precious possession, for unlike wealth or health it can never be regained once lost. Every great achievement springs from time used wisely; successful people are not those given more hours, but those who use their equal share with purpose and discipline. Wasting time through laziness and delay ruins individuals and societies alike, letting opportunities slip away forever. To value time, then, is to value life itself, since life is merely the sum of our hours.

Words: 80 (original ≈ 232 · one-third ≈ 77)

Why it scores: the three illustrations (scholar, athlete, nation) are collapsed into one phrase, "every great achievement"; the proverb about procrastination is dropped as decorative; and the four-paragraph reflection is rebuilt as four flowing clauses — possession, use, waste, conclusion — in fresh words.

Solved précis 4 — "The Habit of Reading"

A second practice passage, on an education theme that pairs with the "investment in knowledge" essay.

Question · reduce to one-third and give a suitable title (10)

Reading is one of the noblest habits a person can cultivate, for it opens doors that no other activity can. Through books, a reader can travel to distant lands, live in other ages, and share the thoughts of the greatest minds who ever lived, all without leaving the room. A good book is a faithful companion that never deserts us and asks nothing in return.

The benefits of reading are many. It enriches our knowledge and widens our understanding of the world. It improves our language, our vocabulary and our power of expression. Above all, it develops the imagination and teaches us to think for ourselves, sharpening the mind as exercise strengthens the body. A person who reads widely is rarely lonely and never truly poor in spirit.

In our own time, however, the reading habit is in danger. The endless distractions of television, mobile phones and social media leave little patience for the quiet attention that a book demands. Many young people now scroll through fragments of information but rarely read a book from cover to cover, and their minds are the poorer for it.

It is therefore vital to revive the love of reading, especially among the young. Parents, teachers and libraries must work together to place good books in young hands, for a nation of readers is a nation of thinkers.

Model Answer

Title: The Habit of Reading

Reading is among the noblest habits, for through books a reader can explore distant places and times and share the wisdom of great minds. It enriches knowledge, improves language and expression, and develops imagination and independent thought. Today, however, this habit is threatened by the distractions of television, phones and social media, so that the young absorb fragments but seldom finish a book. Reviving reading, especially among the young, is therefore vital, for a nation of readers is a nation of thinkers.

Words: 82 (original ≈ 230 · one-third ≈ 77)

Why it scores: the "faithful companion" image and the simile of exercise are sacrificed as ornament; the long list of benefits is condensed to "knowledge, language, imagination and independent thought"; and the closing maxim is retained because it carries the passage's whole argument.

2 Module Two

Letter writing

Three letter types, three registers

MPSC tests a narrow, predictable set. Learn these three and you can handle almost any prompt:

  • Official / Government letter — from one office to another (e.g. an officer reporting to a Director). Most formal. Carries a letter number and an office heading.
  • Formal letter to an authority or business — complaint/grievance, enquiry, application (to an Executive Engineer, a Bank Manager). Formal, no letter number.
  • Letter to the Editor — your views to a newspaper. Formal but more expressive; ends with your name and locality.

Whatever the type, the marks live in the same four places: the correct format, a sharp subject line, a body in three short movements (why I write → the detail → what I want), and a clean, formal tone. Keep it tight — most letters need only 180–300 words.

Format A · Standard formal / business letter (India)

Layout — sender → date → recipient → subject → body → close
[Sender's name]
[House/Street, Locality (Veng)]
[City – PIN]

[Date — e.g. 14 June 2026]

To,
   [Recipient's designation]
   [Organisation]
   [Address]

Subject: [One-line statement of purpose]

Sir / Madam,

   ¶1 — why you are writing (the situation).
   ¶2 — the detail / facts / your view.
   ¶3 — the specific request or action wanted.

Yours faithfully,
[Signature]
[Name in capitals]

Layout — office heading → letter no. + date → recipient → subject → reference → body → close with designation

Format C · Letter to the Editor

Same skeleton as Format A; recipient is "The Editor"; sign with name + locality
To,
   The Editor,
   [Name of the newspaper],
   [City].

Subject: [The issue / your stand on it]

Sir,

   ¶1 — "Through the columns of your esteemed daily, I wish to…"
   ¶2–3 — your views, with reasons.
   Close — appeal to readers / authorities.

Yours faithfully,
XYZ
ABC Locality (Veng), Aizawl
Format do's and don'ts
  • "Yours faithfully" when you do not know the person's name (Sir/Madam). "Yours sincerely" only when you address them by name. In exams, faithfully is almost always correct.
  • Use the placeholder identity the question gives you (here, XYZ of ABC Locality (Veng), Aizawl) — never invent or use a real name/address.
  • In an official letter the writer's identity is the designation, not a home address — so the office heading and the "Sd/- (Name), Designation" close carry it.
  • One idea per paragraph. No slang, no contractions ("don't" → "do not"), no exclamation marks.

Solved letter 1 — Official report (illegal mining)

Prompt: As Assistant Controller of Mines, Siaha, report illegal mining to the Director, Commerce & Industries Dept., Govt. of Mizoram — situation, steps taken, and a request for manpower and technological support.

Model Answer · Official letter 
GOVERNMENT OF MIZORAM
OFFICE OF THE ASSISTANT CONTROLLER OF MINES
SIAHA DISTRICT
No. ACM/SIA/MIN-11/2026-27/47 Dated Siaha, the 14th June, 2026
To,
   The Director,
   Commerce & Industries Department,
   Government of Mizoram,
   Aizawl.
Subject: Report on unauthorised mining activity in a remote area of Siaha District and request for additional manpower and technological support.
Sir,
   I wish to bring to your kind notice that illegal mining activity has been reported from a remote, forested area under the jurisdiction of this office. On the basis of these reports, a preliminary verification was carried out by this office, which has confirmed the unauthorised extraction of minerals and their transportation out of the area by road, without any valid lease, permit or transit pass.
   The following steps have so far been taken at this level. A field inspection was conducted and the site of extraction was identified and photographed; the quantum of disturbed land and the routes used for transportation have been documented; the matter has been brought to the notice of the local administration and the police for immediate ground-level vigilance; and the operators concerned have been verbally cautioned to cease all activity pending action.
   However, effective enforcement is being hampered by certain constraints. The terrain is remote and difficult to access, the extraction appears to continue largely after dark, and this office is presently working with very limited field staff and without modern monitoring equipment. In the absence of round-the-clock surveillance and reliable tracking, the operators are able to evade periodic physical checks.
   It is therefore requested that the Department may kindly sanction additional manpower — field inspectors and enforcement personnel — for this district, and extend technological support such as drones for aerial surveillance of inaccessible sites, GPS-based tracking to monitor the movement of mineral-carrying vehicles, and a digital weighbridge/check-gate facility on the exit route. These measures will enable this office to detect, deter and curb the illegal activity effectively.
   An early decision in the matter would be highly appreciated, given the continuing loss of public mineral resources and the environmental damage involved.
Yours faithfully,
Sd/- XYZ
Assistant Controller of Mines,
Siaha District.
Body ≈ 300 words · official register

Why it scores: the three movements are unmistakable — report → action taken → request — exactly mirroring the prompt's three asks; the request is concrete (drones, GPS, check-gate), not vague; and the close states the public interest (loss of resources, environment) to justify urgency. Note: the exam's placeholder "XYZ of ABC Locality, Aizawl" is used as the signing name; in an official letter the identity is properly the designation, so the home locality is not added to the signature block.

XYZ

Body ≈ 240 words · formal, firm but courteous

Why it scores: the impact paragraph is specific (water, study, medicines, livelihoods, the elderly) rather than a generic "great inconvenience" — concrete consequences are what persuade. The tone is firm but never angry, and the ask is single and clear.

Solved letter 3 — To the Editor (the home-stay trend)

Prompt: With more people taking short, budgeted vacations for work–life balance, the "home stay" option is spreading. Share your views in a letter to the editor.

Model Answer · Letter to the editor
To,
   The Editor,
   The Aizawl Post,
   Aizawl.
Subject: The rise of home stays — a welcome trend that deserves careful nurturing.
Sir,
   Through the columns of your esteemed daily, I wish to share my views on a trend that is fast reshaping the way we travel. As more people seek a healthy work–life balance, short and budget-friendly vacations are replacing long, expensive holidays, and the "home stay" — a paying stay in a family home — has emerged as the natural answer to this demand.
   To my mind, this is a thoroughly positive development, and one of particular promise for a state like ours. Home stays offer the traveller an affordable, authentic experience and the warmth of local hospitality that no hotel can match. More importantly, they put tourism income directly into ordinary households, create self-employment for women and young people, and help preserve our cuisine, crafts and customs by giving visitors a reason to value them. For Mizoram's scenic villages, they open a path to growth that does not require large investment.
   That said, the trend must be nurtured with care. Basic standards of cleanliness, safety and fair pricing should be maintained, hosts may benefit from simple training and registration, and the cultural and environmental character of our villages must be protected from over-commercialisation.
   If guided sensibly by the authorities and the community, the home-stay movement can become a model of responsible, inclusive tourism. I hope your readers and the concerned departments will give it the encouragement it deserves.
Yours faithfully,
XYZ
ABC Locality (Veng), Aizawl
Body ≈ 250 words · views with local colour

Why it scores: a letter to the editor must take a stand and argue it. This one does — clear thesis ("a positive development"), three reasons, one fair caveat (which shows balance and maturity), and a forward-looking appeal. The Mizoram angle makes it feel genuine rather than generic.

Skeleton — To the Editor (potholes in the community)

This prompt is the same type as the home-stay letter, so reuse that exact format and simply re-aim the content. Build it from this skeleton:

Subject: Appeal for urgent attention to the dangerous condition of roads in [locality]
  • ¶1 (open): "Through the columns of your esteemed daily, I wish to draw the attention of the concerned authorities to the deplorable, pothole-ridden state of the roads in our community."
  • ¶2 (the problem, with specifics): craters worsened by the monsoon; daily traffic jams; damage to vehicles; danger to two-wheelers, the elderly and schoolchildren; accidents and waterlogging.
  • ¶3 (neglect): repeated complaints; temporary patch-up that washes away; no lasting repair.
  • ¶4 (appeal): request the authorities, through the newspaper, to undertake permanent repair/resurfacing before more harm is done.
  • Close: Yours faithfully, XYZ, ABC Locality (Veng), Aizawl.
3 Module Three

Essay writing

How to write a strong 1200-word essay

A 1200-word essay is roughly 6–7 paragraphs. That is the whole secret of planning it: a clear introduction, four-to-five body paragraphs each carrying exactly one idea, and a conclusion. Decide those 4–5 ideas before you write, and the essay almost writes itself.

The word budget

PartWhat it doesWords
IntroductionHook → context → your thesis & road-map~150
Body ¶1First main idea + example/evidence~180
Body ¶2Second idea + example~180
Body ¶3Third idea + example~180
Body ¶4Fourth idea / counter-view / India–Mizoram angle~180
Body ¶5The way forward / solutions~170
ConclusionRestate thesis, synthesise, forward-looking close~150
Total≈ 1190

The method — plan, write, polish

  1. Choose by material, not by appeal. Pick the topic for which you can list five solid points and two examples in two minutes — not the one that "sounds" easiest. A topic you can structure beats a topic you merely like.
  2. Make the spine first (5 min). In the margin write your thesis (your one-line answer) and the 4–5 body-paragraph headings in logical order. This is the difference between a 60% essay and an 80% one.
  3. Open with a hook, not a definition. A short scene, a fact, a striking statistic, or a relevant quotation — then narrow to context, then state your thesis and what the essay will argue.
  4. One idea per paragraph, each beginning with a topic sentence, then explanation, then a concrete example (Indian/Mizoram/global), then a link to the next point. Use connectives — furthermore, however, consequently, by contrast — to make the logic visible.
  5. Show balance. A mature essay acknowledges the other side and answers it. Examiners reward nuance over one-sided enthusiasm.
  6. Land the conclusion. No new arguments. Restate the thesis in fresh words, draw the threads together, and end on a forward-looking line or an apt quotation.
What separates a top essay
  • Specifics. Name schemes, places, figures, thinkers — "the National Mental Health Programme", "Finland", "Benjamin Franklin". Vague essays read as empty.
  • A through-line. Every paragraph should serve the thesis. If a paragraph doesn't, cut it.
  • Clean, varied sentences. Mix short and long. Avoid repetition and filler ("In today's world, it is a well-known fact that…").
  • Legible handwriting and clear paragraph breaks — presentation is silently marked.

Model essay 1 — The Role of Artificial Intelligence in Modern Society

Model Essay · ~1200 words

A century ago, the idea of a machine that could hold a conversation, diagnose a disease, or drive a car belonged firmly to the realm of fantasy. Today it is the quiet reality humming behind our smartphones, our hospitals, our banks and our classrooms. Artificial Intelligence — the capacity of machines to perform tasks that once demanded human intelligence, such as learning, reasoning and decision-making — has stepped out of the laboratory and into the fabric of daily life. It is, by common consent, the most transformative technology of our age, comparable in its sweep to electricity or the printing press. Yet its rise forces upon us a single, weighty question: will Artificial Intelligence elevate human society, or quietly erode it? The answer, this essay argues, lies not in the technology itself but in the wisdom and responsibility with which we choose to govern it.

The most visible role of AI is in the ordinary convenience it has woven into everyday life. The voice assistant that sets an alarm, the map that reroutes around a traffic jam, the platform that recommends the next film, the filter that catches spam, the app that translates one language into another in real time — all are AI at work, so seamlessly that we scarcely notice it. By absorbing the small, repetitive labours of the day, AI returns to ordinary people something precious: time and attention for higher pursuits.

Beyond convenience, AI is reshaping the great sectors on which society depends. In healthcare, it reads scans and detects tumours earlier than the human eye, accelerates the discovery of new drugs, and brings expert diagnosis to remote regions through telemedicine. In education, adaptive systems tailor lessons to each learner's pace, a promise of real value for a state like Mizoram where good teachers cannot reach every distant village. In agriculture, AI-guided "precision farming" advises on weather, soil and pests, helping the farmer raise yields while using less. In banking it detects fraud in an instant, and in governance it powers the e-services, grievance systems and data analysis that make administration faster and more transparent. Few corners of modern life remain untouched.

This transformation carries profound economic consequences. By automating routine work and multiplying productivity, AI is generating wealth and entirely new industries and professions. But the same force that creates also displaces: clerical, repetitive and predictable jobs are most exposed, and the fear of large-scale unemployment is real and reasonable. History suggests that technology ultimately creates more work than it destroys — yet that comfort is cold for the individual whose skill is suddenly obsolete. The task before society is therefore not to resist automation but to prepare for it through reskilling, lifelong learning and an education system that teaches the creativity, judgement and empathy machines cannot replicate.

For all its promise, AI brings serious dangers that must be faced honestly. It feeds on personal data, raising grave concerns about privacy and the rise of surveillance. Because it learns from human data, it can inherit and even amplify human prejudices of caste, gender and race, delivering biased decisions under a false cloak of neutrality. It can manufacture "deepfakes" and flood the public square with convincing falsehood, corroding truth and trust. An over-reliance on it can dull human skill and judgement. And it deepens the digital divide, for those without access to technology and connectivity risk being left further behind — a particular worry for the hill regions of the North-East. Above all looms the question of accountability: when an autonomous system causes harm, who is responsible — the coder, the company, or the machine?

A danger that deserves particular attention is the threat Artificial Intelligence poses to truth itself. The same technology that can compose an essay or paint a portrait can fabricate convincing "deepfake" videos of leaders saying what they never said, generate floods of false but plausible text, and tailor propaganda to each individual's fears. In an era when democracy depends on a shared sense of fact, this is deeply corrosive: when citizens can no longer tell the real from the fabricated, trust in institutions, in journalism and in one another begins to dissolve. For a diverse country like India, where a single rumour can ignite real-world violence, the weaponisation of AI-generated falsehood is not a distant hypothetical but a present and pressing risk that law, technology and an informed public must together confront.

Artificial Intelligence has also become an arena of intense global competition, a race in which nations vie for advantage much as they once raced for industrial or nuclear supremacy. Those who lead in AI will shape the standards, reap the economic rewards and command strategic influence in the decades ahead; those who lag risk a new form of dependence. India, with its vast pool of technical talent, its digital public infrastructure and initiatives such as the IndiaAI Mission, is well placed to be a maker of this future rather than merely its consumer. But leadership must be matched by purpose. The aim cannot be technological prowess for its own sake, but AI harnessed for the country's real needs — in healthcare for the underserved, in education for the remote, in agriculture for the small farmer — so that the dividends of this revolution reach the village and not only the boardroom.

The way forward, then, is neither blind embrace nor fearful rejection, but careful, ethical stewardship. We need robust regulation that protects privacy and fixes responsibility; transparency, so that the workings of these systems can be examined and challenged; and a firmly human-centric design, in which AI assists human judgement rather than replacing it. India has begun to move in this direction — through the IndiaAI Mission, investment in skilling, and a stated commitment to "AI for all" — and such efforts must be matched by inclusivity, so that the benefits reach the village as surely as the city. Technology must be bent to serve human dignity, not the other way round.

Artificial Intelligence, in the end, is a tool of extraordinary power — and like every tool from fire to the atom, it is neither good nor evil in itself. The plough and the sword were forged from the same iron; what mattered was the hand that wielded it. AI can heal the sick, teach the child and feed the nation, or it can entrench inequality, erode privacy and spread deceit. The outcome will be decided not in the circuits of the machine but in the choices of the society that builds and uses it. If we approach it with foresight, ethics and a determination that it should serve all and not a few, Artificial Intelligence may well become the finest instrument humanity has ever fashioned for its own progress.

Model essay 2 — The Importance of Mental Health Awareness

Model Essay · ~1200 words

The World Health Organization has long insisted that health is "a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being, and not merely the absence of disease". For generations, however, the world heard only the first word of that definition. We treated the body and ignored the mind; we counted broken bones but never broken spirits. Mental health — our emotional, psychological and social well-being, which shapes how we think, feel and cope — remained a thing whispered about, if it was spoken of at all. Today, amid rising stress, social upheaval and a quiet epidemic of anxiety and depression, that silence has become a luxury we can no longer afford. Mental health awareness is not a soft concern or a passing fashion; it is essential to individual well-being and to the health of society itself, and the time to prioritise it is now.

To understand why, we must first see how vast the issue is. Mental health is not merely the absence of mental illness; it is the foundation of a person's ability to work, to learn, to love and to weather hardship. And mental ill-health is staggeringly common: the WHO estimates that hundreds of millions of people worldwide live with depression and anxiety, and that depression is among the leading causes of disability on earth. These are not rare afflictions of the few but ordinary struggles of the many — neighbours, classmates and colleagues whose suffering is invisible precisely because we have never learned to see it.

This is exactly why awareness matters so deeply. When people understand mental health, they can recognise the early signs of distress in themselves and others, before a crisis arrives. Awareness dissolves the stigma that keeps the suffering silent, and silence is the true enemy: a person who believes their pain is shameful will hide it until it overwhelms them. Most urgently, awareness saves lives, for many who die by suicide were never reached by help they did not know to seek and feared to ask for. To speak openly of mental health is, quite literally, to open a door.

The need is all the more pressing because modern life is straining the human mind as never before. The relentless pace of competition, the pressure of examinations and careers, financial insecurity, and the loneliness that has grown even as we are more "connected" than ever — all take their toll. Social media, for all its uses, breeds comparison and a corrosive sense of inadequacy in the young. In our own region, the scourge of substance abuse, which both feeds on and feeds untreated mental distress, has devastated countless families. These pressures fall heaviest on the youth, who stand at the very threshold of their lives.

The pressures of the digital age deserve a closer look, for they have given the crisis a new and distinctly modern face. Social media, designed to capture and hold attention, draws the young into endless comparison with the carefully curated images of others' lives, breeding inadequacy, envy and a corrosive fear of missing out. The constant connectivity that promises companionship often delivers its opposite — a paradoxical loneliness, and sleep starved by glowing screens late into the night. Cyberbullying pursues its victims into the supposed safety of their own homes, and the dopamine-driven design of apps fosters compulsive use that crowds out rest, exercise and real human contact. For adolescents, whose sense of self is still forming, this environment can be especially destabilising. To take mental health seriously in the digital age is therefore to recognise that the very tools woven into daily life can, left unguided, quietly erode the wellbeing of those who use them most.

It is worth stating plainly, for those tempted to dismiss the issue as a private matter, that the neglect of mental health carries an enormous and measurable cost. Untreated mental illness diminishes productivity, increases absenteeism, and places a heavy burden on families who must care for their loved ones without support or understanding. The economic loss to nations from conditions such as depression and anxiety runs into vast sums, even before the immeasurable human cost in suffering and lost potential is counted. A workforce ground down by stress cannot innovate; a generation of students paralysed by anxiety cannot learn to its full capacity. Investing in mental health is therefore not charity or indulgence but sound economic and social sense — a society that tends to the wellbeing of its people reaps the return in their energy, their creativity and their capacity to contribute.

And yet, against this rising need stand stubborn barriers. The deepest is stigma — the belief that mental illness is a personal weakness, a moral failing, or a matter for shame, so that seeking help feels like an admission of defeat. In close-knit communities, where everyone knows everyone, the fear of being talked about can be paralysing. There is also a severe shortage of trained psychiatrists, counsellors and facilities, especially in rural and hilly areas, so that even those who wish to seek help often cannot find it. Misconceptions abound, and the sufferer is too often told merely to "be strong" or "pray harder", as though a clinical condition were a failure of will.

Confronting this demands action on many fronts at once. Sustained awareness campaigns must normalise conversation about the mind as readily as about the body. Schools and workplaces should embed mental-health education, counselling and supportive environments, reaching people where they spend their days. Care must be made accessible and affordable, and here policy has begun to respond: India's National Mental Health Programme and the Mental Healthcare Act, which recognises mental health as a right, together with the Tele-MANAS national helpline, are real steps toward bringing help within reach. Yet government alone cannot do it. Families must learn to listen without judgement, communities to offer belonging rather than gossip, and each of us to practise the self-care — rest, exercise, connection and the courage to ask for help — that protects the mind.

A society that cares for its minds as faithfully as for its bodies is a more productive, more humane and more resilient society. To break the silence around mental health is not weakness but wisdom; to ask for help is not surrender but strength. The conversation has, at last, begun — and we must not let it fall quiet again. If we can replace stigma with understanding, and neglect with care, we will build a world in which no one suffers alone in the dark, and in which the well-being of the mind is honoured as the foundation of a life truly worth living.

Model essay 3 — "An investment in knowledge pays the best interest"

Model Essay · ~1200 words

"An investment in knowledge pays the best interest." With these words, Benjamin Franklin — printer, scientist and statesman — distilled a truth that has only grown sharper with time. We live today in what is rightly called the knowledge economy, an age in which ideas outrun resources and a single insight can transform an industry overnight. Franklin's metaphor is exact: like money placed in a sound account, knowledge yields a steady, compounding return — but unlike money, it can never be spent away, stolen, or lost to inflation. This essay holds that of all the investments a person or a nation can make, the investment in knowledge is the most valuable and the most enduring, for its returns are reaped not only in wealth but in freedom, dignity and the progress of society itself.

Consider first the force of the metaphor. An ordinary investment carries risk; markets crash, currencies fall, property crumbles. Knowledge does the opposite. Each thing we learn becomes the foundation for learning more, so that understanding compounds upon itself exactly as interest compounds upon capital. A skill, once truly mastered, cannot be confiscated by any thief or any government. It travels with us across borders and through misfortune. This is why the educated mind is the one asset that famine, fire and exile cannot take away — and why Franklin called its interest the "best".

The personal returns of this investment are profound. Knowledge is empowerment: it turns the helpless into the capable and the dependent into the self-reliant. It makes a person employable in a changing world, and adaptable when that world changes again. It sharpens the critical thinking that lets us tell truth from falsehood — a defence more vital than ever in an age drowning in information. And it confers a quiet confidence and dignity, for the man or woman who understands their world walks through it without fear. The returns here are not merely material; they are the very substance of a free and self-respecting life.

What is true of the individual is true, magnified, of the nation. The wealth of modern states rests less on land or minerals than on the knowledge and skill of their people — their human capital. The evidence is written across the map of the twentieth century. Japan and South Korea, poor and war-ravaged, rose to prosperity not on natural riches but on a fierce investment in education, research and technology. Finland built one of the world's finest societies on one of its finest school systems. By contrast, nations rich in oil or ore but poor in learning have too often squandered their fortune, for resources are finite and can be exhausted, while knowledge generates endlessly. The lesson is unmistakable: a country that educates its people invests in a return that never stops paying.

Nor are the dividends only economic. An investment in knowledge purchases a healthier, freer and more just society. Educated citizens make informed voters, and informed voters are the lifeblood of a working democracy; knowledge is the surest guard against manipulation and demagoguery. Education improves public health, lowers infant mortality, advances the status of women, and lifts families out of poverty across generations. Knowledge, in short, is the great leveller — the one form of capital that, once shared, can narrow the very inequalities that money tends to widen.

History shows, too, that knowledge is the truest instrument of liberation. The chains that bind people are often forged of ignorance — of not knowing one's rights, one's worth, or one's possibilities — and education is what strikes them off. It was no accident that reformers and emancipators across the ages placed learning at the very centre of their struggle; Dr B. R. Ambedkar's clarion call to "educate, agitate, organise" put education first, recognising that a people who know cannot long be kept down. The labourer who learns to read, the woman who gains an education, the marginalised community that sends its children to school — each acquires a power that no master can easily revoke. In investing in knowledge, a society invests not merely in skilled hands but in free minds, and free minds are the foundation of every other freedom worth having.

Beyond the individual and the nation, the investment in knowledge is what drives the very progress of the human species. Every advance that has lifted humanity — the conquest of disease, the growth of food, the harnessing of energy, the connecting of continents — began as an investment in understanding, often made long before any practical return was visible. The patient research of scientists whose names most of us will never know, the slow accumulation of insight across generations, is the compound interest of civilisation itself. A society that funds its laboratories, libraries and universities is planting seeds whose fruit may ripen only for its grandchildren — yet it is precisely such far-sighted investment that separates the nations that lead from those that merely follow. To value knowledge is thus to take the long view, and to serve a future one may never live to see.

This truth speaks directly to our own country and our own state. India's much-discussed "demographic dividend" — its vast population of the young — will prove a blessing only if it is matched by an equal investment in their education, skills and health; otherwise it risks becoming a burden of the unemployable. Mizoram, blessed with one of the highest literacy rates in the nation, holds a precious head-start, but literacy is only the foundation; the building must rise upon it through quality, skill and the spirit of lifelong learning. In an age where the half-life of skills grows ever shorter, the investment in knowledge can never be a single deposit made in youth and forgotten; it must be a habit renewed across a lifetime.

A note of caution completes the picture. Knowledge is not the same as the flood of information now within reach of every phone; data without understanding is noise, and information without wisdom can mislead as easily as it informs. The true investment is in discernment — the ability to weigh, to question and to apply — and it must be guided by ethics, lest cleverness be turned to harmful ends. With that caveat, Franklin's verdict stands undimmed. To invest in knowledge is to plant a tree whose fruit ripens for a lifetime and whose seed enriches generations yet unborn. It is the safest of investments, for it cannot be lost, and the most generous, for the more it is shared the more it grows. No wealth a person can amass, and no resource a nation can command, will ever pay an interest so rich, so lasting, or so kind.

Model essay 4 — "The destiny of a nation is shaped in its classrooms"

Model Essay · ~1200 words · MPSC 2024

When the Kothari Commission sat down in 1964 to chart the future of Indian education, it opened its report with a sentence that has echoed ever since: "The destiny of India is now being shaped in her classrooms." It was a profound claim — that a nation's future is written not first in its parliament, its boardrooms or its battlefields, but in the quiet rooms where its children learn. For it is in the classroom that every citizen, leader, scientist, soldier and worker of tomorrow is first formed. The doctor who will heal, the engineer who will build, the voter who will choose and the officer who will govern all begin as pupils at a desk. This essay holds that education is the single most powerful force determining a nation's destiny — its prosperity, its character, its justice and its democracy all flow from the quality of what happens in its classrooms — and that to neglect them is to gamble away the future itself.

Consider first the most measurable truth: the classroom builds a nation's human capital, which in the modern world is its truest wealth. The prosperity of nations no longer rests chiefly on land, minerals or armies, but on the knowledge, skill and creativity of their people. The evidence is written across the last century. Japan and South Korea, devastated and resource-poor, rose to the front rank of nations on the strength of a relentless investment in schooling. Finland built one of the world's most admired societies upon one of its finest education systems. India's own emergence as a global force in information technology was born in the lecture halls of its institutes of technology and science. A classroom that produces capable minds produces a prosperous nation; one that produces only certificates produces stagnation.

Yet a nation is not made of skills alone, and the classroom shapes something deeper than competence — it shapes character. Schools transmit not only knowledge but values: honesty, discipline, civic sense, tolerance, compassion and respect for the law. A nation of educated but unscrupulous people is more dangerous than a nation of the ignorant, for it arms vice with cleverness. As is often said, intelligence plus character is the true goal of education. The classroom that teaches a child to think but not to care, to calculate but not to be just, has failed the nation even as it succeeds at examinations. This is why moral and value education is not a frill but a foundation — the place where the conscience of a citizenry is first formed.

The classroom is also the great engine of equality and social justice. Of all the instruments a society possesses, education is the one most capable of lifting the poor, empowering the marginalised and dissolving the inherited barriers of class, caste and gender. A child born into poverty cannot choose her circumstances, but a good school can change her destiny — and through her, that of her family and her descendants. To educate a girl is to transform a household and a generation. India recognised this when it made education a fundamental right; Mizoram, blessed with one of the highest literacy rates in the country, has already laid this foundation. The classroom, when its doors are truly open to all, is democracy's greatest leveller.

Nor can democracy itself survive without the classroom. A free society depends on citizens able to reason, to weigh evidence, to resist manipulation and to choose their leaders wisely — and these capacities are cultivated in the schoolroom, not granted at the ballot box. The vote in the hand of an unthinking citizen is easily bought or misled; the vote in the hand of an educated one is the foundation of self-government. Education builds the scientific temper and the critical mind that guard a nation against superstition, propaganda and demagoguery. In shaping thinking citizens, the classroom shapes the very quality of a nation's democracy.

All of this, however, rests on one vital condition: the promise of the classroom is realised only when its quality is real. It is not enough to fill schools with children if they leave unable to read, reason or work — and India's persistent "learning crisis", where enrolment has soared but learning has lagged, is a sober warning. Rote memorisation that kills curiosity, a shortage of trained and motivated teachers, the gulf between urban and rural and digital opportunity, and a yawning mismatch between what is taught and what the economy needs — all blunt the classroom's power. The National Education Policy 2020 seeks to answer these ills by emphasising foundational literacy, critical thinking, flexibility and the dignity of skills. The lesson for Mizoram and the nation is clear: the task now is to move from access to excellence, from teaching facts to teaching minds.

At the very heart of the classroom stands the teacher, and no account of a nation's destiny can ignore the one who shapes it day by day. Buildings, syllabi and technology matter, but it is the teacher who breathes life into them — who kindles curiosity, models integrity, and glimpses the promise in a child that the child cannot yet see in herself. A society that honours and prepares its teachers well secures the quality of everything that follows; one that neglects, underpays or overburdens them undermines its own future at the source. The finest curriculum is inert in untrained hands, while a gifted teacher can transform even the humblest schoolroom into a workshop of greatness. To invest in the classroom is therefore, first of all, to invest in the teacher — in their training, their dignity and their own continued learning.

The cost of failing in this duty is not abstract. Where classrooms are starved of attention, the consequences surface a generation later as unemployable youth, a weakened economy, a coarsened public life and a democracy more easily misled — the slow interest charged on an unpaid debt. Conversely, where a society pours its best energies into education, the returns appear in every field at once, for there is no national problem — from poverty to public health to social division — whose solution does not pass, sooner or later, through the schoolroom door.

More than half a century after the Kothari Commission, its insight has lost none of its truth. The destiny of a nation is indeed shaped in its classrooms — written first in pencil at a child's desk before it is inked into the history of a people. A country that fills its classrooms with curiosity, character and competence, and that honours the teachers who tend them, secures its future as surely as any treaty or fortress; a country that neglects them mortgages that future to decline. In the end, the chalk in a teacher's hand shapes the nation more profoundly than the sword in a soldier's or the seal in a minister's. If we wish to know what India and Mizoram will become, we need only look closely at what is happening, today, in their classrooms.

Model essay 5 — "Power tends to corrupt, and absolute power corrupts absolutely"

Model Essay · ~1200 words · MPSC 2024

In 1887, the English historian Lord Acton wrote a sentence in a private letter that has since become one of the most quoted warnings in all of political thought: "Power tends to corrupt, and absolute power corrupts absolutely." He was reflecting on the moral judgement of kings and popes, but his insight reaches far beyond his age. Power — the capacity to impose one's will upon others — is indispensable to any organised society; without authority there is only anarchy. Yet power carries within it a peculiar poison: it tends to erode the conscience of those who hold it, and where it grows absolute and unchecked, that erosion becomes complete. This essay argues that Acton's dictum is borne out by both history and human nature, and that the surest defence against the corruption of power lies not in trusting the virtue of rulers but in building the institutions that limit, check and hold them to account.

Why should power corrupt at all? The answer lies in human psychology. Authority breeds a sense of superiority and entitlement, a feeling of standing above the rules that bind ordinary people. It surrounds the powerful with flatterers who echo their wishes and conceal unwelcome truths, until the ruler hears only his own voice. It dulls empathy, for those who command rarely feel the consequences borne by those they command. Above all, it tempts — for power is the means to satisfy every ambition and appetite. And when power becomes absolute, the final restraint vanishes: the fear of consequence. A leader who answers to no court, no press, no election and no law has nothing left to check the slide from servant of the people into master over them.

History offers a grim and unbroken testimony. Wherever power has been concentrated in too few hands and freed from accountability, the result has been the same — the suppression of dissent, the silencing of critics, the seizure of wealth, and too often the catastrophe of war. The tyrannies and absolute monarchies of the past, and the dictatorships of the twentieth century, all began with a concentration of authority justified in the name of order or glory, and ended in oppression. It is precisely because absolute power has so reliably produced ruin that humanity has repeatedly risen against it, in revolutions that sought to replace the rule of one with the rule of law.

From this hard lesson grew the great political invention of the modern world: the deliberate limiting of power. The philosopher Montesquieu argued that liberty could be preserved only by separating the powers of government, so that each might check the others. Constitutional democracy is built upon this wisdom. It assumes, prudently, that rulers may be corruptible, and so it cages power within institutions — a separation of the legislature, executive and judiciary; an independent judiciary and a free press; periodic elections that can dismiss the powerful; a federal division of authority; and laws, such as the Right to Information, that drag governance into the sunlight. The genius of democracy is not that it produces saints, but that it endures even when it does not.

Acton's warning, moreover, is not confined to thrones and palaces; power corrupts at every level where it goes unchecked. The petty official who demands a bribe, the local strongman who bends the rules, the office-bearer who serves himself before the public — each illustrates the same truth in miniature. This is why a society needs not only grand constitutional checks but everyday mechanisms of accountability: anti-corruption bodies such as the Lokpal and the Central Vigilance Commission, transparent procedures, social audits, and above all an alert and vigilant citizenry. Corruption thrives in secrecy and withers in the open; the remedy is sunlight and the steady insistence that all power must answer for its use.

Among the checks on power, two deserve special mention because they work even between elections: a free press and an active civil society. The press, rightly called the fourth pillar of democracy, shines light into the shadowed corners where the abuse of power prefers to operate; the exposure of a single scandal can topple the mighty more swiftly than any statute. Civil society — citizens organised in associations, movements and watchdog bodies — keeps a continuous pressure on authority that no single election could sustain. When these are strong and free, even great power must walk carefully, knowing it is watched; when they are silenced or captured, the first and most important restraint on power quietly disappears. Their health is, for this reason, a reliable measure of a society's true freedom.

Ultimately, however, no institution can defend a people that will not defend itself. Constitutions can be subverted, courts pressured and laws ignored if the citizenry is indifferent; the strongest safeguard against the corruption of power is a public that values its liberty and refuses to surrender it. This places a quiet responsibility on every citizen — to stay informed, to question authority, to vote with care, and to resist the seductive promise of a strong ruler who asks only that the checks upon him be removed. Tyranny rarely arrives by force alone; more often it is invited in by those grown weary of the burdens of freedom. To guard against the corruption of power is, in the end, a civic duty as much as a matter of constitutional design.

And yet the dictum is a warning, not an iron law of nature. Power need not corrupt the person of true character; in the right hands it can ennoble as readily as it debases. History also honours those who wielded great authority and used it to serve — leaders who surrendered power when they might have clung to it, and who treated their office as a trust rather than a possession. This is the spirit in which power ought to be held, and it is the very ideal placed before the civil servant, who is granted authority over the lives of citizens precisely so that he may serve them. The lesson, then, is twofold: a healthy society cultivates leaders of conscience and builds strong institutions to restrain those who lack it. Neither virtue nor vigilance alone is enough.

Lord Acton's warning endures because the human nature it describes endures. Power will always tempt, and the temptation grows with the power. The question every society must answer is whether it will rely on the goodness of whoever happens to hold authority, or on the strength of the checks it places around them — and the wise answer is to insist upon both. So long as power is treated as a trust, exercised in the open and held firmly to account, it can build roads, deliver justice and serve the common good. The moment it becomes absolute, secret and unanswerable, it begins its descent into corruption. Liberty, it has rightly been said, is preserved only by eternal vigilance — and that vigilance is the price a free people must always be willing to pay.

Model essay 6 — Development versus Environment

Model Essay · ~1200 words · MPSC 2023

For much of the modern age, "development" and "environment" have been set against each other like rivals in a contest, as though a nation must choose between the factory and the forest, the highway and the river, prosperity now and survival later. Behind this supposed conflict lies one of the defining questions of our century: can humanity continue to grow and prosper without destroying the very planet that makes growth possible? This essay argues that the opposition between development and environment is, in the long run, a false one. Development that devours its environment is not progress at all but borrowed ruin, paid for by future generations; genuine, lasting development and a healthy environment are not enemies but partners, and the road that joins them is sustainable development.

It would be dishonest to deny that a real tension exists. Development demands energy, land, minerals, industry and infrastructure, and these have too often come at a heavy environmental price — forests cleared for projects, rivers fouled by effluent, communities displaced by dams, and species lost forever. For a poor nation, the pressure to grow is urgent and immediate: people need jobs, roads, electricity and incomes today, and to such people environmental concern can sound like a luxury of the comfortable. This is the genuine dilemma that confronts every developing society, and it cannot be wished away with slogans.

And yet, to pursue development while ignoring the environment is ultimately to saw off the branch on which one sits. A degraded environment destroys the very foundations of development itself. Polluted air and water breed disease and drain productivity; the smog that chokes great cities and the groundwater vanishing beneath our farms are not abstractions but daily costs. Climate change, driven by unrestrained emissions, threatens agriculture, multiplies floods and droughts, and endangers the food and water security of millions. Growth that exhausts its resources and poisons its surroundings is not wealth being created but capital being consumed.

But the opposite extreme is no answer either. Poverty is itself one of the greatest enemies of the environment. The desperately poor cut forests for fuel, encroach upon fragile land, and cannot afford the cleaner technologies that the rich take for granted. A nation cannot in justice ask its hungry to guard the trees while their children starve. Conservation that ignores human needs is neither fair nor durable, for an environment protected at the cost of people's dignity will not be protected for long. Development and the relief of poverty are themselves environmental goods, because a secure and educated people is far better placed to care for the earth than a desperate one.

Nowhere is this balance more delicate than in a fragile, biodiversity-rich region like the North-East, and Mizoram in particular. Here the hills are steep and the ecology sensitive: roads, dams and unplanned construction can trigger landslides and scar forests, while the traditional practice of jhum, or shifting cultivation, sustains livelihoods even as it strains the land. For such a region the choice is not whether to develop, but how. Mizoram's true path lies in development that works with the hills rather than against them — eco-tourism that earns from beauty preserved, sustainable and settled farming that protects the soil, and renewable energy that powers growth without smoke. The North-East can become a living model of green development, proving that ecology and economy may rise together.

The reconciliation of these claims has a name: sustainable development, defined by the Brundtland Commission as development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own. It is not a vague aspiration but a practical programme — a shift to clean and renewable energy, the adoption of green and resource-efficient technology, the embrace of a circular economy that wastes less, afforestation to repair what has been lost, rigorous environmental-impact assessment before great projects, and firm principles that the polluter must pay and that caution must guide us where harm is uncertain. India has committed itself to this path through ambitious renewable-energy targets, a pledge of net-zero emissions, and a call for sustainable lifestyles. The choice, properly understood, is never development or the environment, but development through environmental responsibility.

It must also be recognised that this is a global challenge marked by deep inequity. The wealthy nations grew rich by burning fossil fuels for two centuries, and bear the greatest historical responsibility for the warming of the planet; yet it is the poorer nations, which contributed least, that often suffer the harshest consequences — the floods, the droughts, the rising seas. It is neither just nor realistic to ask developing countries to halt their growth in the name of a crisis they did little to create. The principle of "common but differentiated responsibilities", enshrined in global climate agreements, captures this fairness: all must act, but those who have polluted most and can afford most must lead, supporting poorer nations with finance and technology. Environmental protection, in short, cannot be separated from the question of global justice.

Nor does the burden fall on governments and industries alone; the path to sustainability is paved by countless individual choices as well. The ways we consume, travel, waste and build all leave their mark upon the earth. A culture of mindless consumption — of buying, using and discarding without thought — drives much of the pressure on the planet's resources, and it is a culture each of us has the power to refuse. Simple habits multiplied across millions of people become a mighty force: conserving energy and water, reducing and recycling waste, planting trees, choosing public transport, and valuing what we have over what we are constantly urged to want. Sustainable development, properly understood, is not only a matter of policy but of values — a quieter, more responsible way of living that treats the earth as a shared inheritance rather than a resource to be plundered.

In the end, the framing of "development versus environment" is itself the error; the wiser framing is development with the environment, each sustaining the other. We are not the owners of the earth but its trustees, holding it briefly in our hands for the generations still to come. A river poisoned for a quick profit and a forest felled for a single season are not wealth but theft from our own children. True development must be measured not by the figures of national income alone, but by the cleanliness of our air, the life in our rivers and the habitability of the world we pass on. Develop we must, and develop we shall — but in a manner the earth can bear, so that our progress endures into the future rather than consuming itself in the present.

Model essay 7 — Political instability in Myanmar and its impact on Mizoram

Model Essay · ~1200 words · MPSC 2024

The maps drawn by governments do not always match the maps drawn by history and kinship. Across Mizoram's eastern frontier lies Myanmar, a nation long troubled and, in recent years, plunged into deep turmoil — and what unfolds there does not remain confined within its borders. For Mizoram, joined to the peoples beyond that frontier by ties of blood, language and a shared past, instability in Myanmar is not distant foreign news but a neighbour's crisis arriving at its own doorstep. This essay argues that the upheaval in Myanmar carries profound humanitarian, security, economic and diplomatic consequences for Mizoram, and that the state's response — caught between the pull of compassion and the weight of complexity — offers a revealing study in how a small border society absorbs the shocks of a neighbour's collapse.

To understand the impact, one must first grasp the situation across the border. Myanmar has endured long stretches of military rule and persistent ethnic conflict throughout its modern history. The political crisis that followed the military's seizure of power in 2021 reignited widespread unrest and armed resistance, and the resulting conflict has caused severe humanitarian distress and large-scale displacement. The Chin State of Myanmar, which directly adjoins Mizoram and is home to peoples closely related to the Mizos, has been among the regions affected. It is from this neighbouring turmoil that the consequences for Mizoram flow.

The most immediate impact, and the heart of the matter for Mizoram, is humanitarian. As conflict has driven people from their homes, many have sought refuge across the border in Mizoram. Crucially, a large number of them are not strangers but kin — members of the wider Zo family of peoples to which the Mizos themselves belong, sharing language, ancestry and culture. Moved by this profound sense of brotherhood, and by the cherished Mizo value of tlawmngaihna, or selfless service to others, Mizo society, its churches and its civil organisations have extended shelter, food and care to those who have fled. For Mizoram, the crisis is felt first not as a question of policy but as a duty owed to one's own.

Yet this generosity brings real and difficult challenges. Mizoram is a small state with limited resources, and a sustained influx places pressure on its infrastructure, its schools, its health facilities and its finances. Questions arise over the registration, shelter, livelihood and long-term future of the displaced. There is, too, a delicate tension between the humanitarian instinct that prevails at the local and state level and the responsibilities of immigration and foreign policy that rest with the central government — a tension sharpened by the fact that India is not a party to the international refugee convention and lacks a settled domestic refugee law. These are matters on which reasonable people and different levels of government may differ, and they must be navigated with both sensitivity and clarity.

Alongside the humanitarian dimension runs a security and border dimension that cannot be ignored. The boundary between Mizoram and Myanmar is long, rugged and porous, and the arrangements that have traditionally allowed the people of the border to move freely have come under review in light of the instability. A neighbouring conflict raises genuine concerns — the possible spillover of armed activity, and the risk that trafficking in arms and drugs may exploit the disorder. The challenge for the authorities is to secure the frontier responsibly without severing the deep human and economic ties that have always bound the two sides, for a border sealed in fear can wound a community as surely as a border left unguarded can endanger it.

There is, finally, an economic and strategic dimension. The instability disrupts the long-standing cross-border trade on which border communities depend, and it clouds the future of important connectivity projects that pass through this region and form part of India's wider Act East policy. Mizoram and the nation thus share a deep, long-term stake in the return of stability to Myanmar. The way forward calls for a balanced and humane approach: a clear and compassionate policy toward the displaced, close coordination between the state and central governments, a calibrated management of the border that protects without isolating, humanitarian support strengthened by national and international assistance, and patient diplomacy aimed at peace and stability in the neighbourhood.

To grasp why this crisis touches Mizoram so deeply, one must understand that the border drawn between India and Myanmar cut through a single people. The Mizos and the Chin, along with related groups, belong to the wider Zo family, sharing a common ancestry, closely related languages and a living cultural kinship that long predates the modern boundary. Families on either side are quite literally relatives; the line on the map is, to them, an administrative fact rather than a true division of identity. This is why the suffering across the border is felt in Mizoram not as the plight of foreigners but as the distress of one's own brothers and sisters — and why the state's response has been so instinctively generous. It is a powerful reminder that the bonds of culture and blood often run deeper than the borders of states.

The episode also illustrates a larger truth about India's place in its neighbourhood: the nation's own security and prosperity are bound up with the stability of the lands around it. A turbulent border is a standing source of refugees, trafficking and insecurity, while a peaceful and prosperous neighbourhood opens the way to trade, connectivity and friendship — the very logic behind India's Act East policy and its patient regional diplomacy. It underscores, too, the importance of cooperative federalism, for a border state like Mizoram, with its unique ethnic ties and intimate local knowledge, has both a stake and a perspective that the central government would do well to heed. The handling of a distant frontier, in other words, is not the concern of Delhi alone, but a partnership between the centre and the states that live with its consequences.

For Mizoram, the crisis unfolding across the border is a test of both conscience and capacity. To turn away kinfolk in their hour of distress would betray the deepest values of Mizo society; yet to bear an open-ended crisis entirely alone would overwhelm a small and limited state. The answer must therefore lie in blending the heart and the head — compassion guided by sound policy, generosity supported by the strength of the wider nation, and security pursued without cruelty. Mizoram alone cannot resolve the turmoil of its neighbour, but it can demonstrate how a humane and well-governed border society responds when a neighbour's house is ablaze: neither slamming its door against those who flee, nor allowing the fire to spread unchecked. The destinies of the Zo people on both sides of that frontier remain, as they have always been, deeply and inseparably intertwined.

Frameworks for the other nine topics

Good governance
Thesis: Good governance — accountable, transparent, participatory and citizen-centred administration — is the bedrock on which development and democracy stand.
  • Define it via its pillars: rule of law, transparency, accountability, responsiveness, participation, equity, efficiency.
  • Why it matters: converts policy into welfare; builds public trust; curbs corruption; ensures last-mile delivery.
  • Tools today: e-governance, RTI, Citizen's Charters, social audits, Direct Benefit Transfer, grievance redress.
  • Challenges: corruption, red tape, weak accountability, the urban–rural and digital divide.
  • Way forward: administrative reform, technology with a human face, decentralisation, an ethical and empowered civil service.
  • Close: good governance is not a destination but a continuous duty — "minimum government, maximum governance" rightly understood.
Ethics in the workplace
Thesis: Workplace ethics — honesty, integrity, fairness and responsibility on the job — is not a constraint on success but its true foundation.
  • What it covers: integrity, accountability, fairness, respect, confidentiality, conflict-of-interest avoidance.
  • Why it matters: trust, reputation, teamwork, long-term sustainability; for public servants, it is the guarantee of citizens' rights.
  • Threats: corruption, favouritism, harassment, dishonesty, the pressure to cut corners for short-term gain.
  • Special weight in public service: probity, impartiality, dedication to public interest (echo the Nolan principles / the civil-service values).
  • Building it: ethical leadership by example, codes of conduct, whistle-blower protection, training, accountability.
  • Close: ethics is doing right when no one is watching — the quiet difference between a job and a vocation.
Education in Mizoram
Thesis: Mizoram's remarkable literacy is a foundation, not a finish line; the next leap is from access to quality, skills and employability.
  • The achievement: among India's highest literacy rates; strong community and church role; near-universal schooling.
  • The gaps: rural–urban quality divide, teacher shortages in remote areas, limited higher/technical education, a mismatch between degrees and jobs.
  • Pressing issues: unemployment among the educated, migration for study and work, infrastructure and connectivity in the hills.
  • Opportunities: NEP 2020, digital and skill-based learning, vocational training, local-language and culturally rooted curricula, eco/agro-based skilling.
  • Way forward: invest in quality and teachers, link education to local livelihoods, expand higher education and entrepreneurship.
  • Close: education as the surest engine for Mizoram's self-reliant, dignified future.
Social & Religious Obligations vs Professional Obligations
Thesis: These obligations need not be rivals; the mature individual harmonises them, and where they truly clash, principle and proportion must guide the choice.
  • Frame both: social/religious ties give identity, belonging and moral grounding; professional duty gives purpose, livelihood and service to society.
  • The tension: festivals vs deadlines; community expectations vs office rules; for a public servant, impartiality vs personal/communal loyalty.
  • The danger of imbalance: neglecting either erodes the self; favouring kin over duty breeds nepotism and injustice.
  • Principles for resolving conflict: integrity, fairness, the public interest, time-management, communication, and conscience.
  • Examples: granting leave for genuine obligation vs refusing to bend rules for community pressure.
  • Close: balance, not sacrifice of one for the other, is the goal — duty performed with humanity.
Effects of screen time & digital overconsumption on the developing minds of Mizo youth
Thesis: Digital technology is a powerful servant but a dangerous master; for Mizo youth, unchecked screen time is quietly shaping minds, health and culture — and demands conscious balance.
  • The reality: smartphones and the internet now central to study, work and social life; rapid adoption among the young.
  • The harms: shortened attention and weaker memory; sleep loss; anxiety, comparison and low self-esteem from social media; sedentary health problems; addiction and reduced face-to-face connection.
  • Cultural dimension: erosion of language, traditional games, community life and outdoor culture; exposure to harmful content; vulnerability to online scams.
  • The other side: access to knowledge, skills, opportunity and global connection — the divide is not screens but how they are used.
  • Solutions: digital literacy, parental and school guidance, screen-time discipline, promoting sport/reading/community, and channelling youth toward creating with technology, not merely consuming it.
  • Close: raise a generation that masters the device rather than being mastered by it.
Creating a prosperous, just and harmonious India
Thesis: True nation-building rests on three legs at once — prosperity, justice and harmony; pursued together they reinforce one another, pursued singly they collapse.
  • Prosperity: inclusive growth, jobs, infrastructure, the demographic dividend, "Atmanirbhar"/self-reliant development that reaches the last person.
  • Justice: social, economic and political justice (echo the Preamble); reducing inequality, empowering the weak, equal opportunity, rule of law.
  • Harmony: unity in diversity; communal, linguistic and regional amity; the special place of integrating the North-East and the aspirations of every region.
  • How they interlock: prosperity without justice breeds unrest; justice without harmony fragments; harmony without prosperity stagnates.
  • The path: education, good governance, equitable development, constitutional values, active and ethical citizenship.
  • Close: the India of our Constitution's promise — one that grows together, shares fairly and lives as one.
Instilling ethical values in youth: forging virtuous citizens through moral education
Thesis: A nation's future is shaped less by what its youth know than by what they value; moral education is therefore not optional but foundational.
  • Why values matter now: rising materialism, corruption, intolerance and a crisis of character amid material progress.
  • The core values: honesty, integrity, compassion, responsibility, tolerance, respect, civic sense, patriotism.
  • The agents of moral education: family first, then school, religion/community, and increasingly the media and peer culture.
  • How to instil them: not by sermons but by example, stories, role models, experiential learning and service; integrating value-education into schooling (NEP's emphasis on values).
  • The obstacles: a "marks-only" education, weak role models, and a media culture that often glorifies the wrong things.
  • Close: educate the head and the heart together — for, as is often said, "intelligence plus character is the goal of true education".

Ten more predicted topics — full frameworks

Based on the recurring MPSC pattern and the strong local relevance of certain themes, here are ten further topics I would bet on, each as a ready-to-expand spine. Several (drugs, tourism, climate in the hills, agriculture) carry a deliberate Mizoram angle — examiners reward an essay rooted in the candidate's own context.

1 · Corruption: the biggest obstacle to national development
Thesis: Corruption is the single greatest enemy of development and democracy — draining resources, eroding trust and crushing the poor; rooting it out is the precondition for genuine progress.
  • Forms: bribery, nepotism, embezzlement, misuse of office; petty (everyday) versus grand (high-level) corruption.
  • The harm: diverts public money from welfare, weakens institutions, deters investment, deepens inequality, and erodes faith in democracy.
  • Causes: greed, low accountability, red tape, excessive discretionary power, weak enforcement, opaque political funding.
  • Tools: RTI, Lokpal/Lokayukta, the CVC, e-governance and Direct Benefit Transfer (less human interface), whistle-blower protection, electoral reform.
  • The deeper cure: ethical education, value-based leadership, citizen vigilance, a culture that shames corruption rather than tolerating it.
  • Close: development without integrity is a house built on sand — change must begin with each citizen.
2 · Social media: a boon or a bane?
Thesis: Social media is a powerful double-edged tool — a force for connection, awareness and empowerment, yet also for misinformation, addiction and division; its worth depends entirely on how we use it.
  • The boon: connects people across distances; democratises information and gives the ordinary citizen a voice; aids education, business, activism and disaster response.
  • The bane: rumour and misinformation; addiction and wasted time; anxiety, comparison and harm to mental health, especially among youth; cyberbullying, privacy loss and scams.
  • Social cost: polarisation, echo chambers and threats to communal harmony — a real danger in sensitive regions.
  • The balance: the medium itself is neutral; responsibility lies with the user.
  • Way forward: digital literacy, self-regulation, fact-checking, parental and school guidance, and sensible platform regulation.
  • Close: make social media a servant, never become its slave.
3 · Women's empowerment as a driver of social change
Thesis: No society can advance while half its members are held back; women's empowerment is not charity but the very engine of national development.
  • Meaning: equal rights and opportunity, education, economic independence, decision-making power, and freedom from violence.
  • Why it matters: empowered women raise healthier, better-educated families; their workforce participation lifts the economy; they bring fresh perspectives to leadership and governance.
  • The Mizoram angle: relatively high female literacy and social standing, yet real gaps remain in political representation and economic leadership.
  • Obstacles: patriarchy, unequal opportunity, violence, the double burden of home and work, and under-representation in decision-making.
  • The path: education, skilling and financial inclusion, legal protection, political reservation, and changing mindsets within the home.
  • Close: "to educate a woman is to educate a family" — empowering her empowers the whole nation.
4 · Drug abuse among the youth: a challenge for Mizoram
Thesis: Drug abuse has grown into one of the gravest threats facing Mizoram's youth and families, demanding a response that is compassionate yet determined, from society and state alike.
  • The reality: proximity to trafficking routes, rising addiction among the young, and the heavy toll on health, families and the social fabric.
  • Causes: peer pressure, unemployment and idleness, stress and untreated mental distress, easy availability, and weak support systems.
  • Consequences: ruined health and lives, crime, broken families, lost human potential, and the spread of disease.
  • The response so far: community- and church-led initiatives, rehabilitation, awareness drives, and enforcement against trafficking.
  • Way forward: prevention through education, employment and recreation; accessible de-addiction and counselling (link to mental health); treating addicts as patients, not criminals; and community vigilance.
  • Close: to save the youth from drugs is to save Mizoram's future.
5 · Climate change and its impact on the fragile North-East
Thesis: Climate change is no longer a distant threat but a present danger, and the ecologically fragile North-East, Mizoram included, stands among the most vulnerable; adaptation and action can no longer wait.
  • What it is: long-term shifts in temperature and weather, driven largely by human greenhouse-gas emissions.
  • Impact on the region: erratic rainfall and prolonged dry spells, landslides and flash floods in the hills, threats to agriculture, water and biodiversity-rich forests, and hardship for farming and jhum-dependent communities.
  • Wider stakes: melting glaciers, rising seas, extreme weather, and food and water insecurity worldwide.
  • Causes to address: fossil fuels, deforestation, and unsustainable land use.
  • The response: mitigation (clean energy, afforestation, reduced emissions) and adaptation (resilient farming, disaster preparedness) — local effort backed by global cooperation.
  • Close: the hills cannot wait — to protect our environment is to protect ourselves.
6 · Tourism as an engine for Mizoram's economic growth
Thesis: Blessed with natural beauty, a rich culture and a peaceful society, Mizoram holds in tourism a sustainable engine for jobs and growth — provided it is developed wisely and responsibly.
  • The potential: scenic hills, festivals, a unique culture and cuisine, warm hospitality, adventure and eco-tourism, and the rise of home stays.
  • The benefits: employment and self-employment, income for rural households, a market for local crafts and produce, and cultural pride and exchange.
  • The challenges: infrastructure and connectivity, accommodation and amenities, marketing, skill gaps, and protecting environment and culture from over-commercialisation.
  • Way forward: invest in roads and air links and facilities; promote eco- and community-based tourism and home stays; train youth in hospitality; market Mizoram as a safe, green destination.
  • The balance: tourism that respects nature and tradition can lift the economy without harming what makes Mizoram special.
  • Close: turn Mizoram's beauty into its livelihood — sustainably.
7 · Population: India's greatest asset or its greatest challenge?
Thesis: India's vast population is neither blessing nor curse in itself; managed wisely through education and jobs it becomes the world's greatest workforce, but neglected it becomes an unbearable burden.
  • The asset view: the "demographic dividend" — a young, large workforce, a vast market, and human capital that can power growth.
  • The challenge view: pressure on jobs, resources, food, housing, health and education; unemployment and inequality if growth lags behind.
  • The deciding factor: investment in education, skills, health and employment turns sheer numbers into productivity.
  • India's position: now the world's most populous nation, with only a narrow window to reap the dividend before the population ages.
  • Way forward: quality education and skilling, job creation, healthcare, and balanced, sustainable development.
  • Close: people are India's greatest resource — but only if India invests in its people.
8 · Unity in diversity: India's enduring strength
Thesis: India's astonishing diversity of language, religion, region and culture is not a weakness to be feared but the very source of its strength and identity.
  • The diversity: many faiths, hundreds of languages, countless customs, and the distinct cultures of regions like the North-East — yet one nation.
  • The unity: a shared Constitution, common values, deep interdependence, and a composite culture — "unity in diversity" as the national idea.
  • Why it is a strength: tolerance, cultural richness, resilience, and the living proof that differences can coexist.
  • The threats: communalism, regionalism, linguistic chauvinism, separatism, and the politics of division.
  • How to preserve it: constitutional values, education for tolerance, equitable development of all regions, and genuine respect for the North-East and every community.
  • Close: India's unity is a garden of many flowers — its diversity is both its beauty and its bond.
9 · Skill development for a self-reliant (Atmanirbhar) India
Thesis: In a competitive, fast-changing world a degree alone no longer guarantees a livelihood; skilling India's youth is the key to employment, dignity and a truly self-reliant nation.
  • The problem: educated unemployment, a gap between what schools teach and what the economy needs, and an over-reliance on government jobs.
  • Why skilling matters: it makes youth employable and entrepreneurial, powers industry, agriculture and services, reduces dependence, and builds an Atmanirbhar Bharat.
  • Initiatives: Skill India, vocational training, NEP 2020's vocational focus, and the promotion of entrepreneurship.
  • The Mizoram angle: channel youth into hospitality, agro-processing, handicrafts, IT and tourism-linked skills suited to local strengths.
  • Way forward: industry-linked training, quality vocational education, support for start-ups and self-employment, and respect for the dignity of all labour.
  • Close: a skilled youth is a self-reliant nation — teach a skill, secure a future.
10 · Agriculture and the future of Mizoram's economy
Thesis: Agriculture remains the backbone of Mizoram's economy and the livelihood of most of its people; modernising it sustainably is essential for food security, jobs and rural prosperity.
  • The reality: a largely agrarian society, the long tradition of jhum (shifting) cultivation, and rich potential in horticulture and cash crops.
  • The challenges: the low productivity of jhum, soil erosion and deforestation, dependence on the monsoon, poor market access and storage, and the drift of youth away from farming.
  • The opportunities: settled and organic farming, horticulture (fruits, flowers, bamboo), allied activities (piggery, poultry, fisheries), and food processing and value addition.
  • Way forward: a shift from jhum to sustainable settled cultivation; irrigation, technology and credit; roads and markets; and making farming profitable and respectable for the young.
  • Wider tie: a strong rural economy curbs migration and underpins overall development.
  • Close: invest in the soil that feeds Mizoram — its future grows from the land.

Six frameworks the pattern demands

These fill the clearest gaps the past papers expose. Together with the twenty-five frameworks and seven full essays above, they cover every dominant family on the MPSC record.

1 · Higher education versus skill acquisition (and NEP 2020)
Thesis: Higher education and skilling are not rivals but partners; a nation needs both the thinker and the doer, and NEP 2020 rightly seeks to bridge the two.
  • The debate: degrees confer knowledge, research capacity and prestige; skills confer immediate employability and self-reliance.
  • The Indian problem: a flood of unemployable degree-holders alongside industries starved of skilled hands — a deep mismatch between education and the job market.
  • Why both matter: society needs scientists and scholars and equally needs technicians, artisans and entrepreneurs; the dignity of skilled labour must be restored.
  • NEP 2020's answer: vocational exposure from school, flexibility and multidisciplinary learning, and the blurring of the "academic versus vocational" divide.
  • The Mizoram angle: channel youth toward skills suited to local strengths — hospitality, agro-processing, handicrafts, IT — while keeping higher education accessible.
  • Close: educate the mind and train the hand — a nation needs both to prosper.
2 · The challenge before the civil servant today
Thesis: The modern civil servant stands at the crossroads of rising public expectation and mounting complexity; meeting today's challenges demands not only competence but integrity, empathy and adaptability.
  • The changed context: an aware, demanding and connected citizenry; rapid technological change; complex cross-cutting problems — climate, pandemics, security.
  • The challenges: the pressure to be transparent and accountable; resisting political and other undue pressure; ethical dilemmas and corruption; last-mile delivery; keeping pace with data and technology.
  • The qualities required: integrity and impartiality, empathy for the weak, efficiency, lifelong learning, and the courage to uphold the law and the public interest.
  • The regional dimension: serving diverse, remote and sensitive areas like Mizoram with cultural sensitivity and a developmental drive.
  • The way forward: ethical grounding, capacity-building, and citizen-centric, technology-enabled administration.
  • Close: the civil servant is the steel frame of the nation — and its conscience must be as strong as its competence.
3 · Development versus environment
Thesis: Development and environment are too often cast as enemies, but lasting development cannot come at the cost of the environment that sustains it; the goal is not one over the other, but sustainable development.
  • The tension: industry, roads, dams and growth set against forests, rivers, clean air and biodiversity — jobs today versus survival tomorrow.
  • Why it is a false choice in the long run: a poisoned environment ultimately destroys the very health, livelihoods and growth it was meant to serve.
  • The cost of ignoring the environment: pollution, climate change, deforestation and displacement — with a heavy toll on fragile regions like the North-East.
  • The cost of ignoring development: poverty, unemployment and want, which are themselves drivers of environmental harm.
  • The reconciliation: sustainable development, clean and renewable energy, eco-tourism, afforestation, and holding the earth in trust for future generations.
  • Close: we have not inherited the earth from our ancestors but borrowed it from our children — develop, but do not destroy.
4 · Political instability in Myanmar and its impact on Mizoram
Thesis: The turmoil across Mizoram's eastern border is no distant foreign affair but a matter of direct humanitarian, security and economic consequence, demanding both compassion and careful management.
  • The background: prolonged instability and conflict in Myanmar following its political upheaval.
  • The human dimension: an influx of refugees sharing close ethnic and cultural kinship (the Chin–Mizo bond), and Mizoram's humanitarian response.
  • The challenges: pressure on local resources, border management and security, the risk of cross-border crime and trafficking, and the delicacy between humanitarian duty and national policy.
  • The ties and opportunities: shared kinship and history, and the potential of cross-border trade under the Act East policy.
  • The way forward: a humane refugee policy, firm but compassionate border management, centre–state coordination, and long-term stability through diplomacy.
  • Close: as neighbours bound by blood and history, Mizoram cannot look away — its response must blend the heart and the head.
5 · Media and social responsibility (the power of the press)
Thesis: A free press is the lifeblood of democracy and a great power in society; but power without responsibility is dangerous, and the media's freedom must be matched by its conscience.
  • The power: the "fourth pillar" of democracy — it informs citizens, shapes opinion, exposes wrongdoing and holds power to account.
  • The responsibilities: truth and accuracy, fairness and balance, sensitivity, and the public interest placed above profit or sensation.
  • The dangers today: sensational, TRP-driven news; paid and fake news; bias and propaganda; trial by media; and the chaos of unregulated social media.
  • The social-media dimension: everyone is now a publisher, so power — and responsibility — have spread to the ordinary citizen.
  • The way forward: ethical journalism, media literacy among citizens, self-regulation, and protecting press freedom from both state and commercial capture.
  • Close: with great power comes great responsibility — a free press must also be a fair and truthful one.
6 · Tradition versus modernity in a changing Mizo society
Thesis: Tradition and modernity need not be at war; a wise society modernises without losing its soul, drawing strength from its roots even as it reaches for the future.
  • The change: globalisation, technology, education and new aspirations are rapidly transforming Mizo society.
  • What is at stake: language, customs, community life (the spirit of tlawmngaihna), crafts, dress and values — the very markers of identity.
  • The pull of modernity: better living standards, opportunity, knowledge and connection — real goods that cannot and should not be refused.
  • The danger of extremes: blind tradition that stifles progress, and rootless modernity that erodes identity and leaves the young adrift.
  • The balance: preserve and adapt — keep culture alive through education, the arts and conscious practice, while embracing the tools of the modern world.
  • Close: a tree grows tall only because its roots run deep — Mizoram can be modern and remain itself.

The exam-hall checklist

Précis

  • Roughly one-third length, and the word count is written at the end
  • One continuous paragraph, in your own words, no headings or points
  • Third person, neutral tone; no opinion, no new matter
  • A short, relevant title at the top

Letter

  • Correct format for the type; sharp one-line subject
  • Body in three movements: why → detail → request
  • Formal tone, no contractions or slang; correct close ("Yours faithfully")
  • Signed with the given placeholder identity, not a real name

Essay

  • A 90-second skeleton made before writing; clear thesis
  • 6–7 paragraphs, one idea each, with topic sentences and connectives
  • Specific examples, names, figures, a quotation; a balanced view
  • Conclusion restates the thesis and ends on a forward-looking line — no new arguments
  • Legible hand, clear paragraph breaks, time kept for a final read-through
