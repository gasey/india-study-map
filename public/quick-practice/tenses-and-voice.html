English does not have a separate tense for every situation. It has three times — past, present, future — crossed with four aspects — simple, continuous, perfect, perfect continuous. Twelve combinations. Learn the grid and you never memorise a list again.

How to read every entry

The four aspects, in plain terms

Simple A fact, a habit, or a completed whole. It does not fuss about duration. “Water boils at 100°C.” “She works in Imphal.”

Continuous Action in progress, unfinished, temporary. Built with be + -ing. “He is writing.”

Perfect Looks back from a point in time to something already done — the result still matters. Built with have + past participle. “She has finished.”

Perfect Continuous The duration of an action up to a point, often still going. Built with have been + -ing. “They have been waiting for an hour.”

The building blocks — every term, in plain words

Before the twelve tenses make sense, a handful of small words need to click into place: participle, gerund, auxiliary, transitive. None of them are hard. Each one below comes with a plain definition and a real-life picture to make it stick.

The foundation

One verb, three forms (V1 · V2 · V3)

Almost every tense is built by combining a helper word with one of a verb's three forms. Learn these three and the whole system unlocks.

V1 — base The plain dictionary form: go, eat, write, walk. Used for present simple and after to.

V2 — past The simple past: went, ate, wrote, walked. Stands alone, no helper. “I wrote it.”

V3 — past participle The "finished" form, used with have (perfect tenses) and be (passive): gone, eaten, written, walked. “I have written it.” “It was written.”

🔧 Analogy: think of a verb as one Swiss-army knife with three blades. Same tool, but you flick out a different blade for a different job — V1 for the present, V2 for a finished past, V3 whenever a helper like have or be is holding the handle. Regular verbs just add -ed for V2 and V3 (walk/walked/walked). Irregular ones change shape (go/went/gone) and simply have to be memorised.

The word everyone asks about

What is a participle?

A participle is a verb that has stepped out of its "doing" job and is moonlighting as a describing word (an adjective) or teaming up with a helper to build a tense. There are two:

Present participle The -ing form: running, boiling, smiling. It carries a sense of in-progress, alive, happening. It builds every continuous tense (be + -ing: “She is running”) and can describe a noun (“the boiling kettle”, “a crying baby”).

Past participle Usually the V3 form: broken, eaten, written, finished. It carries a sense of done, completed, received. It builds the perfect tenses (have + V3: “She has written”) and the passive (be + V3: “It was written”), and can describe a noun (“a broken cup”).

📸 Analogy: a present participle is a livestream — it catches the action mid-happening (“the running water”). A past participle is the “delivered” stamp on a parcel — the action is over and the result is sitting in front of you (“the broken window”).

The -ing twin

Gerund — the same -ing word, a different job

A gerund is an -ing verb being used as a noun — the name of an activity. It looks identical to the present participle, but it does a noun's work: it can be a subject, an object, or sit after a preposition.

Gerund (a noun): “Swimming is fun.” — here swimming is the thing we're talking about, just like “Tea is nice.” “I enjoy cooking.” — cooking is the object of enjoy.

Present participle (a verb/adjective): “She is swimming.” — here it's part of the verb, the action in progress.

🎭 Analogy: it's the same person wearing two hats. At the office he's “the driver”; at home he's “the father” — one man, the role decides the label. Ask one question: does the -ing word name a thing? → gerund (noun). Does it describe something or show action? → participle.

Two more you'll meet constantly

Infinitive & auxiliary (helping) verbs

Infinitive The verb in its untouched form, usually with to: to go, to eat, to learn. It's the neutral, "to-do-list" version that hasn't been bent to any time yet.

Auxiliary verb A helping verb — be, have, do and the modals (will, can, should…). It carries no real meaning of its own; it sets the tense, makes the passive, or forms questions and negatives. In “She has finished”, has is just the helper; finished carries the meaning.

🎤 Analogy: auxiliaries are the backup singers and stage crew. The main verb is the lead vocalist everyone hears; the helpers quietly set the key, the timing and the lighting so the lead can shine. An infinitive is the recipe title before you start cooking — “to bake”, “to run” — pure intention, no time attached yet.

The piece that makes passive possible

Subject, object & transitive verbs

Subject The doer of the action. Object The receiver of the action. In “The cat chased the mouse,” the cat is the subject (doer), the mouse is the object (receiver).

Transitive verb A verb that needs an object — you must do it to something: eat (food), write (a letter), build (a house). Only transitive verbs can become passive, because the passive promotes the object to the front.

Intransitive verb A verb that stands alone, no object needed: sleep, arrive, laugh, exist. You can't make these passive — there's nothing to promote.

🏐 Analogy: picture a throw-and-catch. The subject is the thrower, the object is the catcher. A transitive verb always hands the ball to a catcher (“She kicked the ball”). An intransitive verb keeps the ball to itself — there's no one to throw to (“She slept”), which is exactly why “was slept” makes no sense.

How the big labels fit together

Tense vs. aspect vs. voice

Tense = WHEN Past, present, or future — which point on the calendar.

Aspect = HOW Simple, continuous, perfect, or perfect continuous — the shape of the action at that time (finished? in progress? looking back?).

Voice = WHO Active (the subject does it) or passive (the subject receives it) — who gets the spotlight.

🎬 Analogy: tense picks the day on the calendar. Aspect picks the camera angle on that day — a snapshot, a slow-motion clip, or a look back at what just finished. Voice decides where the camera points — at the chef cooking (active) or at the dish being cooked (passive). The clues hidden in sentences

Signal (time) words — the road signs of tense

Certain little words quietly point to the right tense. Spotting them is half the battle in any test:

already, yet, ever, never, just, since, for → usually perfect (“I have just finished”).

now, at the moment, right now, look!, listen! → usually continuous (“She is reading now”).

yesterday, ago, last week, in 2010, when → usually past simple (“We met last week”).

always, usually, every day, often, never (habit) → usually present simple (“He always walks to work”).

🚦 Analogy: signal words are road signs telling you which tense lane to be in. “since 2016” is a sign reading “perfect lane ahead”; “yesterday” says “past-simple exit only.”

Going deeper

Six more ideas that decide your tense

Once the building blocks above feel comfortable, these six explain most of the "but why is it wrong?" moments in tense and voice.

Why "I am knowing" is wrong

Stative vs. dynamic verbs

Dynamic verbs Describe an action you can start, do, and stop: run, eat, build, write. These happily take the continuous (-ing) form: “I am eating.”

Stative verbs Describe a state, not an action — thoughts, feelings, senses, possession: know, believe, love, want, own, seem, understand. They normally refuse the continuous: we say “I know”, never “I am knowing.”

🎞️ Analogy: a dynamic verb is a movie — there's motion you can freeze at any frame, so "in progress" (continuous) makes sense. A stative verb is a photograph — it's already a still state; you can't film a photograph in progress. That's why loving, knowing, owning sound wrong in continuous tenses.

Attitude on top of the verb

Modal verbs

Modals are a special set of helpers — can, could, will, would, shall, should, may, might, must — that add an attitude to the main verb: ability, possibility, permission, advice, or obligation. They never change form (no -s, no -ed) and are always followed by the bare base verb.

Ability: She can swim. · Possibility: It might rain. · Permission: You may leave.

Advice: You should rest. · Obligation: I must go. · Future/willingness: I will help.

🧂 Analogy: a modal is seasoning sprinkled on the verb. "He works" is the plain dish; can, should, must, might are different spices — they don't change the dish (work), they change the flavour: how certain, how allowed, how obliged.

How sentences are built

Clauses & connectors

Clause A group of words with its own subject + verb. A main clause stands alone as a full sentence (“She left”). A subordinate clause can't stand alone — it leans on the main one (“…because she was tired”).

Connectors The joining words that link clauses and signal time or logic: and, but, because, although, while, when, before, after, since, as soon as, until. Many of them also fix the tense around them (“before”, “after”, “by the time” often pull in a perfect tense).

🏠 Analogy: a main clause is a house that can stand on its own; a subordinate clause is an extension that collapses without the house to attach to. Connectors are the doorways between rooms — they decide how the ideas connect (cause, contrast, sequence).

The "if" tenses

Conditionals (if-sentences)

Conditionals pair two tenses in a fixed pattern. Each type talks about a different kind of possibility:

Zero Always-true facts. If you heat ice, it melts. (present + present)

First A real future possibility. If it rains, I will stay home. (present + will)

Second An unreal or unlikely present. If I won the lottery, I would travel. (past + would)

Third An impossible past (it didn't happen). If I had studied, I would have passed. (past perfect + would have)

🥤 Analogy: a conditional is a vending machine — "put in X, get Y." The type of "if" just sets which buttons are wired: a zero conditional always dispenses the same can (facts), while a third conditional is a machine for a coin you never actually had (the past you can't change).

When you repeat what someone said

Reported (indirect) speech

When you report someone's words instead of quoting them, the tense usually steps back one step (called backshift), and time/place words shift too.

Direct: “I am tired.” → Reported: She said she was tired. (present → past)

Direct: “I saw it.” → Reported: He said he had seen it. (past → past perfect)

Direct: “I will call.” → Reported: She said she would call. (will → would)

🔊 Analogy: reported speech is an echo — it arrives a moment later and a step lower. The original words bounce back shifted one tense into the past, the way an echo returns slightly changed from what was shouted.

A passive cousin you'll meet

Causative — "have / get something done"

The causative is a passive-flavoured structure for when you arrange for someone else to do something, rather than doing it yourself: have/get + object + past participle (V3).

I had my car repaired. (a mechanic did it — not me)

She got her hair cut. (a stylist did it)

We are having the house painted. (painters are doing it)

🧰 Analogy: the causative is hiring a professional. You don't pick up the wrench yourself — you arrange for the job to be done. Notice the past participle (repaired, cut, painted) is the same "finished" form the passive uses, because the object is receiving the action.

Quick wins

Common mistakes to avoid

❌ “I have seen him yesterday.” → ✅ “I saw him yesterday.” A finished past time (yesterday, ago, last week) needs the past simple, not the present perfect.

❌ “I am knowing the answer.” → ✅ “I know the answer.” Stative verbs avoid the continuous.

❌ “She said she will come.” → ✅ “She said she would come.” Backshift in reported speech.

❌ “The work was did by him.” → ✅ “The work was done by him.” The passive uses the past participle (V3 = done), never the past simple (did).

❌ “He don't like it.” → ✅ “He doesn't like it.” Third-person singular present needs -s / doesn't.

Exam-grade reference

Grammar A–Z — everything else the exams test

Tenses and voice are the backbone, but competitive papers (SSC, Bank PO, CLAT, school boards) also test concord, articles, prepositions, the parts of speech, and a set of question formats. Here is the whole map, rule by rule, with worked examples.

The skeleton

The eight parts of speech

Noun Names a person, place, thing or idea — teacher, Delhi, book, freedom.

Pronoun Replaces a noun — he, she, it, they, who, this.

Verb Shows action or state — run, is, seem, build.

Adjective Describes a noun — tall, blue, honest.

Adverb Describes a verb, adjective or another adverb — quickly, very, well.

Preposition Shows relationship in time/space — in, on, at, by, with.

Conjunction Joins words or clauses — and, but, because, although.

Interjection A sudden exclamation — Oh! Alas! Wow!

🏗️ Analogy: the parts of speech are job titles on a building site. Nouns are the materials, verbs do the work, adjectives and adverbs decorate, prepositions and conjunctions are the joints that hold everything together. A word's job (not its spelling) decides its label — “water the plants” is a verb; “drink water” is a noun.

The #1 error-spotting topic

Subject–verb agreement (concord)

A singular subject takes a singular verb; a plural subject takes a plural verb. The trick is spotting the real subject. Key rules:

1. Each, every, either, neither, one of + noun → singular. “Each of the boys has a pen.”

2. Two subjects joined by and → plural; but a single combined idea → singular. “Bread and butter is healthy.”

3. With either…or / neither…nor / not only…but also, the verb agrees with the nearer subject. “Neither the boys nor the teacher was there.”

4. The number of → singular; a number of → plural. “A number of fans were waiting.”

5. Uncountables and -ics subjects (news, advice, mathematics, physics) → singular.

6. Collective nouns (team, jury, family) → singular as a unit, plural when members act individually.

🎯 Exam tip: a phrase between the subject and verb is a decoy. In “The quality of the products is good,” the subject is quality (singular), not products. Mentally cross out the “of …” phrase and the right verb becomes obvious.

Small words, big marks

Articles — a, an, the

a / an Indefinite — any one of a kind. Choose by sound, not letter: a university (yoo-), a one-rupee coin (wun-), but an hour (silent h), an MBA (em-).

the Definite — a specific or unique thing: the sun, the Ganga, the book I bought, superlatives (the best), and ordinals (the first).

zero article No article with general plurals/uncountables (“Honesty is a virtue”), most proper nouns, meals, languages, and institutions used for their purpose (“go to school/bed”).

🚪 Analogy: a/an opens the door to any room (“I need a chair — any chair”); the points to one specific room you both already have in mind (“sit on the chair by the window”).

The trickiest little words

Prepositions

Prepositions show position, direction, or time — and English fixes many of them by convention. Common ones worth memorising:

Time: at 5 o'clock · on Monday · in May/2026 · since a point · for a period · by a deadline.

Place: at a point · on a surface · in an enclosed space · into (movement in) · onto (movement on).

Fixed pairs: good at, married to, accused of, die of, abide by, prefer X to Y, congratulate on, superior/junior/senior to, composed of, capable of, afraid of.

⚠️ Common traps: discuss / order / request / resemble / marry take no preposition (“discuss the issue”, not “discuss about”). “Returned back”, “repeat again” and “reason is because” are redundant.

Naming things

Nouns — countable, uncountable & number

Uncountable nouns take a singular verb and no plural -s: information, advice, furniture, luggage, news, equipment, scenery, work, money. Say “much advice”, not “many advices”.

Always plural (take a plural verb): scissors, trousers, spectacles, jeans, scales, premises, goods.

Singular-looking plurals: cattle, police, people, poultry take a plural verb (“The police are here”).

🧊 Analogy: countable nouns are ice cubes (you can count them: one cube, two cubes); uncountable nouns are water (you measure it: some water, much water) — you can't say “three waters” unless you mean glasses of it.

Stand-ins for nouns

Pronouns — case & agreement

Subject vs object: use I, he, she, we, they as the doer; me, him, her, us, them after a verb or preposition. “Between you and me” (never “you and I”).

Reflexive (myself, himself) only when the subject and object are the same person: “He cut himself.” Don't use it as a fancy 'me' (“Give it to myself” ✗).

Who vs whom: who = subject (“Who called?”), whom = object (“Whom did you call?”). One is matched by one's, not 'his'.

🪞 Quick test: for who/whom, answer with he/him — if “him” fits, use whom (“You called him” → “Whom did you call?”).

Describers

Adjectives, adverbs & degrees of comparison

Adjective vs adverb: adjectives describe nouns (“a quick runner”); adverbs describe verbs/adjectives/adverbs (“she runs quickly”). After sense verbs (look, smell, taste, feel) use an adjective: “It smells good.”

Three degrees: positive (tall), comparative (taller), superlative (tallest). Use comparative for two things, superlative for three or more.

Don't double up: “more taller” / “most cleverest” are wrong. Use taller / cleverest. Some comparatives take to: senior/junior/superior/inferior/prior to.

📏 Analogy: degrees are medal podiums — positive is just “a tall athlete”, comparative is “the taller of two”, superlative is “the tallest” on the top step. You only need the podium (superlative) when three or more are competing.

How much, how many

Determiners & quantifiers

Countable: many, few, a few, fewer, several, both, each, every. Uncountable: much, little, a little, less, amount of.

Few vs a few: few = almost none (negative), a few = some (positive). Same for little / a little.

Some vs any: some in positive sentences and offers; any in questions and negatives. Each/either/neither take a singular noun and verb.

💡 Tip: use fewer for things you can count (“fewer cars”) and less for quantities you can't (“less traffic”). The supermarket sign “10 items or less” is technically wrong — it should be “fewer”.

Joiners

Conjunctions & correlatives

Coordinating (join equals): for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so (the “FANBOYS”).

Subordinating (start a dependent clause): because, although, since, while, if, unless, until, before, after, as.

Correlative pairs must stay matched: either…or, neither…nor, not only…but also, both…and, no sooner…than, hardly…when, scarcely…when.

⚠️ Frequent errors: “No sooner … than” (not 'then'); “Hardly/Scarcely … when” (not 'than'); don't pair “Although … but” — use one connector, not both (“Although he is rich, he is unhappy”).

Confirming statements

Question tags

A short question tagged onto a statement. The rule: positive statement → negative tag, and vice-versa, repeating the auxiliary.

“She is coming, isn't she?” · “You don't smoke, do you?”

Special cases: “I am right, aren't I?” · “Let's go, shall we?” · “Open the door, will you?”

Words like never, hardly, seldom, no one make a sentence negative, so the tag turns positive: “He never lies, does he?”

The road signs of writing

Punctuation essentials

Comma separates items and clauses; semicolon (;) joins two related full sentences; colon (:) introduces a list or explanation.

Apostrophe: possession (the boy's bag, the boys' bags) and contractions (it's = it is). Its (no apostrophe) is possessive.

Its/it's, your/you're, their/there/they're are the most-tested confusions — learn them cold.

High-level error spotting

Redundancy, parallelism & modifiers

Redundancy / pleonasm: avoid saying the same thing twice — “return back”, “repeat again”, “free gift”, “final conclusion”, “each and every”, “reason is because”.

Parallelism: items in a list must share the same form. ✗ “She likes reading, to swim and cycling.” ✓ “She likes reading, swimming and cycling.”

Misplaced / dangling modifiers: put the describing phrase next to what it describes. ✗ “Walking down the road, the tree was tall.” ✓ “Walking down the road, I saw a tall tree.”

Know the question types

Common exam formats — how to attack them

Spotting errors: a sentence in 3–4 parts; pick the faulty one (or “No error”). Scan for concord, tense, preposition, and article slips first — that's where most errors hide.

Sentence improvement / replacement: a phrase is underlined; choose the best correction or “No improvement”. Read the whole sentence with each option before deciding.

Cloze test: a passage with blanks; choose words that fit grammar and meaning. Read the full passage once before filling.

Para jumbles: reorder scrambled sentences. Find the opening sentence (introduces the topic), then follow pronouns, linkers (however, therefore) and time order to chain the rest.

Word power

Vocabulary — idioms, one-word substitution & confusables

Idioms are fixed phrases whose meaning isn't literal: once in a blue moon (rarely), a piece of cake (very easy), let the cat out of the bag (reveal a secret), burn the midnight oil (study late).

One-word substitution: illiterate (cannot read/write), astronomer (studies stars), aviary (place for birds), democracy (rule by people), omnivore (eats both plants and meat).

Confusables: affect (verb) / effect (noun); principal (head) / principle (rule); stationary (still) / stationery (paper); accept / except; advice (noun) / advise (verb); lose / loose.

Learn it properly

Deep dives — concept first, then heavy practice

Six topics that trip up almost everyone, each taught from the idea up — a mental model, the rules, worked examples — and then drilled with a dedicated quiz that marks you instantly. Pick a topic:

Prepositions

A preposition is a small word that fixes the relationship between a noun (or pronoun) and the rest of the sentence — usually in time, place, or direction. In “the cat sat on the mat”, on tells you where.

🐭 The mouse-and-box model: almost every place preposition is somewhere a mouse can go relative to a box — in the box, on the box, under, over, behind, beside, between two boxes, through a hole. Picture the box and the word becomes obvious.

The core principle

at → on → in : a zoom dial

in The widest view — inside an area or enclosed space, and the longest time units. in India, in the box, in 2026, in May, in the morning.

on Zoom in to a surface, a line, or a single day. on the table, on the wall, on the bus, on Monday, on 5th June.

at The tightest zoom — one exact point or precise clock time. at the door, at the corner, at the bus stop, at 9 o'clock, at noon.

🔍 Analogy: think of a camera zoom. in = zoomed out (the whole area), on = a surface inside it, at = one pinpoint. The same dial works for time: in a year → on a day → at a moment.

Time prepositions

since vs for · by vs until

since + a starting point (since 2010, since Monday). for + a length of time (for two hours, for years).

by = no later than a deadline (finish by Friday). until / till = up to that time, continuously (wait until Friday).

Movement vs position

into / onto / across / through

Static position uses in / on / at. Movement uses directional prepositions: into (entering), onto (moving to a surface), across (one side to the other), through (in one end, out the other), towards (in the direction of), over / under / past.

“The ball is in the box” (position) vs “He put the ball into the box” (movement).

The part you must memorise

Fixed prepositions (collocations)

Many prepositions aren't logical — they're just fixed by usage, like mini-idioms. Learn these as set phrases:

+ of: afraid of, proud of, capable of, accused of, fond of, consist of, die of, composed of.

+ to: married to, similar to, prefer X to Y, senior/junior/superior to, listen to, belong to, object to.

+ for: famous for, responsible for, apologise for, talent/flair for, wait for, look for, hope for.

+ at / on / with / from: good at, congratulate on, insist on, agree with, comply with, suffer from, refrain from.

⚠️ Traps: the verbs discuss, order, request, resemble, marry, reach, enter take no preposition (“discuss the matter”, not “discuss about”). And avoid redundancies: “return back”, “repeat again”, “reason is because”.

Modal verbs

Modals — can, could, may, might, will, would, shall, should, must, ought to — are helping verbs that add the speaker's attitude to the main verb: how possible, allowed, advisable, or necessary something is.

They behave specially: no -s in the third person (“she can”, not “cans”), no -ed, and they're always followed by the bare base verb — never 'to' (except ought to and the semi-modal have to).

✗ She must to go. · ✗ He cans swim. · ✓ She must go. · ✓ He can swim.

🎚️ Analogy: a modal is a dial of strength. For certainty: must (sure) → should (probable) → may/might/could (possible). For obligation: must (have to) → should (advisable) → needn't (no need). For politeness: can → could → would you mind…

What each one does

The main jobs

Ability: can (present), could (past) — “She can drive”, “I could swim at five”.

Permission: may, can, could — “May I leave?”

Possibility: may, might, could — “It might rain”.

Obligation / necessity: must, have to, should, ought to — “You must stop”, “You should rest”.

Prohibition: mustn't — “You mustn't smoke here”. (Note: needn't = no necessity, very different!)

Deduction: must (sure it's true), can't (sure it's false) — “He must be tired”, “That can't be right”.

Looking back

Modal + have + past participle

To talk about the past, add have + V3:

should have + V3 = a past regret/criticism: “You should have told me.”

must have + V3 = a confident guess about the past: “She must have left.”

could have + V3 = an unrealised past possibility: “We could have won (but didn't).”

Connectors

Connectors (conjunctions and linkers) join words, phrases, and clauses, and signal the logical relationship between ideas — addition, contrast, cause, result, condition, purpose, or time. Choosing the right one is all about matching that logic.

🚦 Analogy: connectors are road signs for the reader. “and / moreover” = keep going (addition); “but / however” = sharp turn (contrast); “because / since” = here's the cause; “so / therefore” = here's the result; “if / unless” = a fork in the road.

Three families

Coordinating, subordinating, linking

Coordinating (FANBOYS) join two equal parts: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so. “She tried hard, but she failed.”

Subordinating start a dependent clause: because, although, since, if, unless, while, until, before, after, so that. “Although she tried, she failed.”

Linking adverbs join whole sentences (usually after a full stop or semicolon): however, therefore, moreover, nevertheless, consequently. “She tried hard; however, she failed.”

Match the meaning

Which connector for which idea?

Contrast: but, although, though, whereas, however, nevertheless, despite/in spite of (+ noun).

Cause: because, since, as · + noun: because of, due to, owing to.

Result: so, therefore, thus, consequently, as a result.

Purpose: so that, in order to, so as to.

Condition: if, unless, provided that, as long as.

⚠️ Classic errors: use only one connector — “Although he is poor but he is honest” → “Although he is poor, he is honest”. Watch the pairs: “No sooner … than”, “Hardly/Scarcely … when”, “Not only … but also”. And remember: because + a clause, because of + a noun.

Conditionals (if-sentences)

A conditional has two halves: the condition (the if-clause) and the result (the main clause). There are four standard types, and each is a fixed tense recipe. Get the recipe right and the sentence is correct.

🥤 Analogy: a conditional is a “what-if” machine. The type tells you how real the scenario is: a fact that always works (zero), a likely future (first), a daydream about now (second), or a regret about a past you can't change (third).

The four recipes

Zero · First · Second · Third

Zero — facts if + present, present. If you heat ice, it melts.

First — real future if + present, will + base. If it rains, I will stay in.

Second — unreal now if + past, would + base. If I won the lottery, I would travel.

Third — impossible past if + past perfect, would have + V3. If I had studied, I would have passed.

The rules that catch people out

Two things to never forget

1. No will or would in the if-clause. ✗ “If it will rain…” → ✓ “If it rains…”. The 'will/would' lives in the result half only.

2. Unless = if not. “You'll fail unless you study” means “…if you don't study”. So the result is usually negative or warns of a bad outcome.

💡 Tip: in the second conditional, formal English uses were for all persons — “If I were you…”. Mixed conditionals exist too (past condition → present result): “If I had taken that job, I would be rich now.”

Reported (indirect) speech

Reported speech is how you repeat what someone said in your own words, without quotation marks. Turning direct speech into reported speech involves three changes.

🔊 Analogy: reported speech is an echo — it comes back a moment later, one step lower in tense, and rephrased from your point of view rather than the original speaker's.

The three changes

Tense · pronouns · time & place words

1. Tense backshift — each tense steps back one: present → past, past → past perfect, will → would, can → could, may → might, must → had to. “I am busy” → She said she was busy.

2. Pronouns shift to the reporter's viewpoint: I → he/she, we → they, my → his/her, you → me/him.

3. Time & place words shift: now → then, today → that day, tomorrow → the next day, yesterday → the day before, here → there, this → that, ago → before.

Reporting different sentence types

Statements, questions & commands

Statement: say / tell (that) … — “He said (that) he was tired.”

Yes/No question: ask + if / whether + statement order — “She asked if I liked tea.”

Wh-question: ask + question word + statement order (drop 'do/does/did') — “He asked where I lived” (not 'where did I live').

Command / request: tell / order / ask + to + base verb — “She told me to close the door”; negative: “…not to be late.”

💡 No backshift when the reporting verb is in the present (“She says she is tired”) or when reporting a universal truth (“The teacher said the earth is round”).

Causative

The causative is how you say that you cause or arrange for someone else to do something, instead of doing it yourself. “I repaired my car” = I did it. “I had my car repaired” = someone did it for me.

🧰 Analogy: the causative is delegating like a manager — you don't pick up the tools, you arrange the job. The past participle (repaired, cleaned, built) is the “job-done” stamp the action carries.

The two patterns

Focus on the thing vs the person

1. have / get + THING + past participle (V3) — the usual form; the doer is often unimportant. I had my hair cut. · She got the report printed.

2. + PERSON: have + person + base verb, or get + person + to-infinitive. I had the mechanic check it. · I got the mechanic to check it.

Where the tense lives

Change 'have/get', keep the rest

Only have / get carries the tense; the action verb stays as a past participle (for things). I have it cleaned (present) · I had it cleaned (past) · I will have it cleaned (future) · I'm having it cleaned (now).

Related structures: make + person + base (force): “The teacher made us wait.” · let + person + base (allow): “She let me go.” · help + person + (to) base.

Active & Passive

Who is doing what to whom

Voice is not about correctness — both voices are correct. It is about where you put the spotlight. Active voice points at the doer; passive voice points at the thing done.

  • Active The chef cooked the meal. — subject = doer
  • Passive The meal was cooked by the chef. — subject = receiver

Notice the swap: the active object (the meal) becomes the passive subject. The active subject (the chef) moves to a by-phrase — and is often dropped entirely when it is obvious, unknown, or unimportant.

The conversion in three moves

1. Move the object to the front — it becomes the new subject.
2. Change the verb to the right form of “be” + past participle. The tense of “be” must match the original verb's tense.
3. Put the old subject after “by” — or delete it.

Only verbs that take an object (transitive verbs) can be made passive. “She slept.” has no object, so it has no passive form.

Passive in every tense

The pattern is always be + past participle; only the form of be changes. Continuous tenses use being; perfect tenses use been. (Future continuous and the two perfect-continuous tenses are normally avoided in the passive — they are clumsy, so good writers rephrase.)

When the passive is the right choice

The doer is unknown or irrelevant. “My bike was stolen.” — you don't know who.

The doer is obvious. “The suspect was arrested.” — by the police, naturally.

Science, process & formal writing. “The mixture was heated to 80°C.” — the focus is the procedure, not the person.

To keep the topic in subject position. English likes old information first; the passive lets you front the thing already being discussed.

Special cases worth knowing

Two objects

Verbs like give, send, offer, teach have two objects, so two passives are possible:
Active: They gave me a prize.
Passive A: I was given a prize.  ·  Passive B: A prize was given to me. (Passive A, fronting the person, is more natural.)

Modal verbs

Pattern: modal + be + past participle. “They must finish it.” → “It must be finished.”

Imperatives

Pattern: Let + object + be + past participle. “Close the door.” → “Let the door be closed.”

Reporting verbs

“People say he is rich.” → “He is said to be rich.” / “It is said that he is rich.”
